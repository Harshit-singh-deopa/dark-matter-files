# **What Is Champ?**

Champ is the name commonly given to the legendary lake creature said to inhabit **Lake Champlain**.

Lake Champlain is approximately 120 miles long and extends through New York and Vermont before reaching Quebec.

Descriptions of Champ vary considerably.

Some reports describe:

* a long serpentine body
* several humps
* a horse-like or reptilian head
* a long neck
* dark or black coloration
* a large body beneath the surface
* movement resembling an undulating snake
* an animal swimming rapidly through the lake

Other reports describe something much less dramatic.

Some witnesses have reported only:

* a dark object
* several humps
* an unexplained wake
* a long shape
* something briefly breaking the surface

This variation is important because there is **no single scientifically established description of Champ**.

Even ECHO, the Leahy Center for Lake Champlain, describes Champ as a **cryptid**, meaning a creature whose existence has not been confirmed by science.

# **Lake Champlain: The Setting of the Mystery**

Lake Champlain is a long, narrow freshwater lake situated between the Adirondack Mountains of New York and the Green Mountains of Vermont, with its northern portion extending into Quebec.

The lake is large enough to create enormous distances across open water.

Weather can change quickly.

Wind can create long lines of waves.

Boats frequently travel across the lake.

Large fish inhabit its waters.

Floating logs and other debris can remain partially submerged.

These characteristics are important because many alleged Champ sightings involve objects viewed from considerable distances.

# **The Indigenous Traditions**

Stories of unusual water creatures existed in the region before the modern Champ legend.

Abenaki and Iroquoian traditions have sometimes been connected with the later lake-monster story.

However, these traditions should be treated carefully.

Traditional Indigenous stories have their own cultural and spiritual contexts and should not automatically be rewritten as nineteenth- or twentieth-century "cryptid sightings."

Some popular Champ accounts describe an Abenaki or Iroquoian horned serpent and then directly identify it with the modern monster.

That connection is **not necessarily historically justified**.

The modern Champ legend developed through a mixture of Indigenous traditions, European-American folklore, newspaper reports, tourism and later cryptozoology.

# **The Samuel de Champlain Story**

One of the most repeated claims about Champ says that French explorer **Samuel de Champlain** saw a huge lake monster in 1609.

According to the popular version, Champlain supposedly encountered a creature resembling a giant serpent.

This story appears in countless modern articles.

But there is a major problem.

**Champlain's actual writings do not contain the famous account of him personally seeing a giant twenty-foot monster in Lake Champlain.**

What Champlain actually described was a large fish known locally as the **chaousarou**.

His description included a long snout, sharp teeth, scales and a body resembling a pike.

Modern researchers have suggested that this description fits the **longnose gar**, a real fish native to the region.

# **How the Champlain Monster Story Developed**

The dramatic story of Champlain personally seeing a monster became popular much later.

Research into the history of the Champ legend has found that the famous "20-foot serpent" version does not appear in Champlain's original writings.

Historian Robert E. Bartholomew's work on Champ specifically addresses this problem and describes the popular Champlain encounter as largely fictitious.

This is an important correction for anyone writing about Champ.

The accurate version is:

**Champlain documented a large fish in the region.**

The inaccurate popular version is:

**Champlain personally encountered the modern Lake Champlain Monster.**

Those are not the same thing.

# **The 1819 Captain Crum Report**

The modern historical record becomes much stronger in the nineteenth century.

On **July 22, 1819**, a report appeared concerning a supposed enormous creature seen in Lake Champlain near Bulwagga Bay.

The witness was identified as **Captain Crum**.

A letter published in the *Plattsburgh Republican* described an unusual disturbance on the lake followed by what was reported as a gigantic serpent-like creature.

The account gave an extraordinary estimated length of approximately **187 feet**.

It described the creature as having:

* a dark or black body
* a flattened head
* three teeth
* large eyes
* a reddish band around the neck
* a star-like marking on the forehead
* a long body and tail

The report claimed that the creature moved rapidly before disappearing beneath the surface.

# **How Reliable Was the 1819 Account?**

The report is historically important because it is an early published account.

But historical existence of a newspaper report is not the same as proof that the creature described actually existed.

The enormous size estimate is particularly difficult to evaluate.

The witness was observing something on a large lake, and there was no physical specimen with which to compare the estimate.

The report therefore belongs in the historical record as an **eyewitness claim**, not as confirmed zoological evidence.

# **The 1870s: Champ Becomes a Public Sensation**

During the nineteenth century, reports of strange creatures continued to circulate.

Steamships, railway workers, fishermen and other travelers were among those later associated with Champ reports.

By the 1870s, newspapers were increasingly publishing stories about a mysterious "sea serpent" in Lake Champlain.

This was also the period when the famous showman **P. T. Barnum** became associated with the legend.

# **P. T. Barnum's Famous Reward**

P. T. Barnum is frequently said to have offered a **$50,000 reward** for the capture of the Lake Champlain Monster in 1873.

ECHO, the Leahy Center for Lake Champlain, preserves this part of the Champ tradition and notes the $50,000 reward.

The reward helped transform the creature from a regional story into something that could attract national attention.

People began treating the lake monster as both a mystery and a spectacle.

# **Did Anyone Claim the Reward?**

No verified Champ specimen was ever delivered to Barnum.

No scientifically documented body was produced.

No confirmed skeleton or preserved animal established the existence of a previously unknown species.

The reward therefore became part of the legend rather than the discovery of an actual animal.

# **The Name "Champ"**

The creature eventually became known as **Champ**, a shortened name derived from **Champlain**.

The nickname made the creature more approachable and helped transform it from an anonymous "sea serpent" into a recognizable character.

The name also contributed to Champ's later cultural identity.

Today, Champ appears in:

* local tourism

# **The 1883 Nathan H. Mooney Sighting**

One of the most frequently cited nineteenth-century reports came in **1883**.

**Nathan H. Mooney**, identified in historical accounts as a Clinton County sheriff, reported seeing an unusual water creature.

He described a large serpent-like animal in Lake Champlain.

The reported distance was roughly 20 rods, or about 110 yards.

Mooney reportedly estimated the creature at approximately **25–30 feet long**.

He also claimed that he could see features around its mouth.

# **Why the Mooney Report Became Important**

The Mooney report received attention partly because the witness was a law-enforcement official.

It also helped encourage additional reports.

Once newspapers began circulating stories of a giant creature, more people started paying attention to unusual objects on the lake.

This created a feedback loop:

**one report → newspaper coverage → increased public attention → additional sightings → more newspaper coverage.**

That pattern would become extremely important in the later history of Champ.

# **The Late Nineteenth-Century Sightings**

# **The 20th Century**

Champ reports continued during the twentieth century.

The creature increasingly became part of the folklore of the Lake Champlain region.

Tourism also played an increasingly important role.

The legend was no longer simply a collection of newspaper reports.

It had become a cultural phenomenon.

# **The 1945 S.S. Ticonderoga Report**

One frequently cited report dates to **1945**.

Passengers aboard the **S.S. Ticonderoga** reportedly observed an unusual creature in the middle of Lake Champlain.

Accounts describe a large object or creature moving through the water.

As with earlier reports, no physical evidence was recovered.

The event therefore remains an eyewitness report rather than a confirmed biological observation.

# **The Growing Interest in Scientific Investigation**

By the twentieth century, researchers interested in lake monsters began looking for evidence beyond eyewitness testimony.

The key question changed from:

**"Did someone see something?"**

to:

**"Can we obtain physical evidence that can be independently tested?"**

Researchers began considering:

* photography
* film
* sonar
* underwater observation
* biological evidence
* environmental conditions

This was an important development because photographs and scientific measurements can potentially be independently examined.

# **The 1977 Sandra Mansi Photograph**

The most famous modern Champ event occurred on **July 5, 1977**.

**Sandra Mansi** was visiting the Lake Champlain region with her family.

According to her account, she noticed an unusual object rising from the water.

She took a photograph with a Kodak Instamatic camera.

The resulting image shows what appears to be a dark object rising from the lake.

It has been interpreted by some observers as:

* a head
* a neck
* part of a large body

The image became the most famous photograph associated with Champ.

# **Why the Mansi Photograph Became So Important**

Unlike many earlier reports, the Mansi case provided a visual record.

There was now something that researchers could examine.

The photograph became particularly influential because it appeared to show a large object emerging from the water rather than merely a disturbance or wake.

It was eventually reproduced in newspapers and magazines and became the central image of the Champ legend.

# **The Photograph Was Not Immediately Published**

The photograph was taken in 1977 but became publicly famous later.

According to historical accounts, the image remained in the family's possession before being brought to wider attention.

This delay is one reason the photograph has generated debate.

Supporters have argued that the family had little reason to fabricate the image before the story became famous.

Skeptics have questioned the identification and scale of the object rather than necessarily claiming that the photograph itself was fabricated.

# **Is the Mansi Photograph Fake?**

This question requires careful wording.

There is an important distinction between:

**"The photograph is fake."**

and:

**"The photograph does not prove a monster."**

The second statement is much safer and more accurate.

The photograph is a genuine historical photograph associated with the Mansi family.

Researchers have examined it and debated what it depicts.

There is no scientific consensus identifying the object as a previously unknown animal.

CBS has preserved the photograph in its historical coverage of the case.

# **The Problem of Scale**

One of the biggest problems with the Mansi photograph is determining the object's actual size.

A photograph can show an object clearly without giving a reliable measurement.

To determine scale, researchers need a known reference point.

For example:

* a person
* a boat
* a measured distance
* a known object at the same depth

The Mansi image does not provide a sufficiently reliable reference that allows scientists to determine the object's true dimensions with confidence.

# **Why Location Matters**

The exact location from which the Mansi photograph was taken became difficult to reconstruct.

That created another problem.

Without the exact camera position and exact position of the object, researchers cannot reliably recreate the original geometry.

This makes calculations about the creature's size uncertain.

# **Could the Object Have Been a Log?**

One of the leading skeptical explanations is a **floating or partially submerged log**.

A waterlogged tree trunk can sometimes become positioned vertically or at an angle.

Only part of the trunk may remain visible above the water.

From a distance, this can produce an appearance resembling:

* a neck
* a head
* a hump
* an animal emerging from the water

Some modern analyses have argued that a relatively small object could produce the appearance seen in the photograph.

This is one of the most important alternatives to the monster interpretation.

# **Could It Have Been an Animal?**

It is possible that some Champ sightings involved genuine animals.

Lake Champlain contains several large and unusual-looking species.

These include:

* lake sturgeon
* longnose gar
* other large fish
* otters
* waterfowl

A large fish moving close to the surface can look very different from its appearance underwater.

A group of animals can also create the impression of one long creature.

# **The Longnose Gar Theory**

The longnose gar is especially relevant because of Samuel de Champlain's original description.

Longnose gar have:

* elongated bodies
* long narrow snouts
* sharp teeth
* armor-like scales
* unusual prehistoric-looking appearances

These characteristics help explain why an early observer might have described an unfamiliar gar in dramatic terms.

This does not explain the entire Champ legend.

But it provides a very plausible explanation for at least the fish described in Champlain's writings.

# **The Lake Sturgeon Theory**

Lake Champlain also contains **lake sturgeon**, which can become large.

A large sturgeon near the surface can create a striking disturbance.

When viewed from a distance, its body and movement can potentially be interpreted as something much more unusual.

However, there is no evidence demonstrating that lake sturgeon are responsible for all Champ reports.

This is better understood as a possible explanation for individual sightings.

# **The Plesiosaur Theory**

The most famous cryptozoological theory is that Champ could be a surviving **plesiosaur** or another prehistoric reptile.

Plesiosaurs were extinct marine reptiles.

Some species had:

* long necks
* relatively small heads
* broad bodies
* four flippers

Their appearance resembles the classic image of Champ.

This similarity has made the theory extremely popular.

# **Problems With the Plesiosaur Theory**

The theory has major scientific problems.

Plesiosaurs became extinct approximately 66 million years ago.

Lake Champlain is a relatively young post-glacial lake.

There is also no verified fossil, DNA sample, carcass or living specimen demonstrating that a plesiosaur survived into the modern era.

A breeding population would also have to remain hidden despite centuries of human activity around the lake.

Therefore:

**The plesiosaur theory is a popular explanation in cryptozoology, but there is no scientific evidence establishing it.**

# **The Zeuglodon or Ancient Whale Theory**

Another historical theory proposed an extinct whale-like animal, sometimes identified as **Basilosaurus**, formerly known as Zeuglodon.

This theory was promoted by some cryptozoological researchers because the animal's elongated body could superficially resemble some Champ descriptions.

A famous example came from zoologist **Roy Mackal**, who examined the Mansi photograph and argued in 1980 that the image could represent a primitive whale rather than a plesiosaur. UPI reported his interpretation at the time.

However, this was an interpretation of the photograph—not proof that such an animal lived in Lake Champlain.

# **The Giant Eel Theory**

Eels are another possible explanation for some lake-monster sightings.

A large eel moving close to the surface can produce an elongated, serpentine appearance.

However, there is no verified evidence that Lake Champlain contains eels of the enormous sizes required by the most dramatic Champ reports.

Therefore, the giant-eel theory is possible as an explanation for some ambiguous observations but does not establish the existence of Champ.

# **The Multiple-Animals Theory**

Another possibility is that some reports involve several ordinary animals.

For example, a group of:

* otters
* waterfowl
* fish

can create a sequence of moving shapes.

If the animals appear and disappear beneath waves, the observer may interpret them as several humps belonging to one enormous creature.

This explanation could account for some reports describing multiple humps.

# **The Boat-Wake Theory**

Boat wakes are another important explanation.

A wake can appear as a series of large moving humps.

From a distance, the boat producing the wake may be difficult to see.

The observer may see only the water disturbance.

If the wake moves at an unusual angle relative to the observer, it can resemble a long animal moving through the lake.

# **The Floating-Log Theory**

A floating log is particularly relevant to Champ because of the famous Mansi photograph.

A partially submerged tree trunk can create a shape that looks surprisingly biological.

Waterlogged wood can also move slowly and unpredictably.

If waves lift one end of a log, the visible shape can change from moment to moment.

This could explain why an observer might report something that appeared to rise and then disappear.

# **Optical Illusions and Atmospheric Effects**

Large bodies of water can produce visual distortions.

Changing light, reflections, mist, waves and atmospheric conditions can alter how an object appears.

Distance can also dramatically affect perception.

A small object close to the observer may appear larger than a larger object farther away.

This is one reason eyewitness estimates of Champ's length vary so dramatically.

# **The Expectation Effect**

There is another important possibility.

Once a population becomes familiar with a monster legend, unusual observations may increasingly be interpreted through that legend.

A person seeing an unidentified object may initially think:

**"What is that?"**

But someone who already knows the Champ story may think:

**"Could that be Champ?"**

That difference can affect how an experience is remembered and reported.

# **The 1977 Photograph and Later Sightings**

Research into Champ sightings has identified an interesting pattern.

A 2023 statistical reanalysis examined more than **300 reported sightings** and found that reports increased substantially after the Mansi photograph became public.

The increase was especially noticeable during the years following publication and later diminished toward earlier levels.

This does not prove that the later witnesses were lying.

Instead, researchers have suggested that publicity may have influenced attention and interpretation.

The famous photograph effectively gave people a visual template for what Champ was supposed to look like.

# **The Social History of Champ**

Champ is therefore more than a cryptid story.

It is also an example of how legends develop.

A typical cycle can look like this:

**Local tradition → unusual sighting → newspaper report → public interest → additional sightings → famous photograph → tourism → scientific investigation → renewed media coverage.**

Each stage can influence the next.

This is why historians studying Champ are interested not only in whether a creature exists, but also in **how the legend itself developed**.

Robert E. Bartholomew's book *The Untold Story of Champ* specifically examines Champ as a social and cultural phenomenon.

Instead, many reports can be explained by ordinary phenomena without assuming an unknown species.

# **What Scientists Can Actually Say**

Science cannot currently identify Champ as a real biological species.

There is no accepted zoological classification for the creature.

There is no type specimen.

There is no DNA sequence.

There is no verified skeleton.

There is no confirmed carcass.

There is no independently verified physical evidence demonstrating an unknown giant animal.

ECHO's educational exhibit therefore presents Champ within the category of cryptids—animals whose existence has not been scientifically confirmed.

# **The Most Reasonable Conclusion**

The Champ mystery is best understood as a combination of **folklore, genuine eyewitness reports, ambiguous observations, famous photography, cultural expectation and unresolved individual cases**.

There is currently no scientific evidence establishing a large unknown animal living in Lake Champlain.

At the same time, it would be inaccurate to say that the entire Champ story was invented from nothing.

There really are historical reports.

There really is a famous photograph.

There really are people who have reported seeing unusual things.

The unresolved question is what those people actually saw.

# **Why Champ Continues to Fascinate People**

Champ has survived because it occupies an interesting space between folklore and science.

If the creature had been conclusively identified as an ordinary log decades ago, the story would probably have disappeared.

If a living unknown animal had been captured, the mystery would have ended.

Instead, the evidence remains somewhere between those extremes.

There are reports but no specimen.

There is a photograph but no definitive identification.

There are theories but no confirmed explanation.

That uncertainty is what keeps Champ alive.

# **Final Conclusion**

The **Lake Champlain Monster, known as Champ**, is one of North America's most famous lake-monster legends.

Its history is much older and more complicated than the familiar story suggests.

Indigenous traditions around the region contain their own water-creature traditions, but those traditions should not simply be rewritten as modern cryptozoological reports.

Samuel de Champlain's genuine 1609 writings describe a remarkable large fish, not the famous twenty-foot monster he is often said to have personally encountered. That part of the Champ story was added much later.

The more reliable modern historical record begins in the nineteenth century.

The **1819 Captain Crum report** described an enormous serpent-like creature.

The **1883 Nathan H. Mooney report** added another prominent eyewitness account.

P. T. Barnum's association with the story helped transform the creature into a national curiosity.

But the event that ultimately defined Champ was the **1977 photograph taken by Sandra Mansi**.

The photograph remains fascinating because it is a genuine historical photograph whose subject has never been conclusively identified. It may show an animal, a floating object, a log or another natural phenomenon. It does not, however, provide enough information to establish the existence of a previously unknown species.

More than 300 Champ reports have been compiled in historical and research collections, but a 2023 statistical reanalysis found that sightings increased considerably after the Mansi photograph became widely known. This suggests that publicity and expectation may have influenced at least part of the later sighting record.

The most popular theories include a surviving prehistoric reptile, an ancient whale, a giant eel, a large fish, a group of animals, floating logs, boat wakes and optical illusions.

Of these, the prehistoric-animal theories face particularly serious biological problems.

No verified Champ specimen has ever been recovered.

No skeleton has been confirmed.

No tissue sample has established a new species.

No DNA evidence has confirmed a giant unknown animal.

No photograph has conclusively identified Champ.

Therefore, **Champ remains a cryptid rather than a scientifically recognized animal.**

The most responsible conclusion is not that every sighting was false, nor that the monster has been proven real.

It is that people have repeatedly reported unusual things on Lake Champlain, some of those reports remain unexplained, and the evidence collected so far has not established the existence of a large unknown creature.

That uncertainty is precisely what has allowed Champ to remain one of America's most enduring lake mysteries.
