## **The Hangzhou Airport UFO Incident at a Glance**

On the evening of **July 7, 2010**, something unusual was reported in the airspace around **Hangzhou Xiaoshan International Airport** in Zhejiang Province, China.

The report was not simply made by someone looking at the night sky from their backyard.

A flight crew preparing to land reported an unidentified object to air traffic control. Airport authorities responded by temporarily suspending operations. Incoming flights were diverted to other airports, while departing flights were held.

In total, **18 flights were affected**.

The airport stopped normal operations at approximately **8:45 p.m.** and resumed them at approximately **9:41 p.m.** Contemporary reporting said 12 incoming flights were diverted and six outgoing flights were delayed. More than 2,000 passengers were reportedly affected.

The incident immediately became a national and international news story.

Then the photographs started appearing.

Some showed strange lights. Others showed apparently enormous objects. Videos supposedly showing the Hangzhou UFO also began circulating online.

There was just one major problem.

**Many of those photographs and videos were not connected to the airport incident at all.**

A later joint investigation by UFO researchers from Beijing and Shanghai found that the most widely circulated photographs showed ordinary aircraft and that the airport's own unidentified object had **not been captured in an official photograph or video released to the public**.

That distinction completely changes the story.

The airport shutdown was real.

The unidentified aerial report was real.

The viral "UFO photographs" were a different matter.

And the identity of the object that actually caused the airport to close was never publicly established with certainty.

## **Where Did It Happen?**

The incident took place at **Hangzhou Xiaoshan International Airport**, serving Hangzhou in Zhejiang Province, eastern China.

The airport is located in the Xiaoshan District, southeast of central Hangzhou.

It was a major commercial airport, meaning that an unexplained object entering its airspace was treated as a genuine aviation-safety issue rather than simply an unusual UFO report.

That is one reason the case attracted so much attention.

The authorities did not need to decide whether the object was extraterrestrial.

They only needed to decide whether it posed a danger to aircraft.

For that reason, the airport closure was a precautionary aviation decision.

## **What Happened on July 7, 2010?**

According to contemporary reports, the first important sighting occurred at around **8:30–8:40 p.m.**

A flight crew preparing to land at Xiaoshan reported seeing a strange, twinkling object.

ABC News reported that a flight crew preparing for descent spotted the object at approximately **8:40 p.m.** and alerted air traffic control.

China Daily reported that airport personnel said a twinkling object had first been noticed around **8:30 p.m.** and that the object did not appear on airport radar.

The exact minute varies slightly between reports, but the important point is consistent:

**The airport received a report of an unidentified aerial object shortly before operations were suspended.**

Authorities did not know what it was.

Because the object was reportedly in or near controlled airspace, airport officials took the safest option.

They stopped aircraft operations.

## **The Airport Was Shut Down**

At approximately **8:45 p.m.**, Xiaoshan Airport suspended operations.

The runway and associated air traffic operations remained affected until approximately **9:41 p.m.**

The closure lasted roughly an hour.

During that period:

**Six outbound flights were delayed.**

**Twelve inbound flights were diverted.**

The incoming aircraft were redirected toward nearby airports, including **Ningbo and Wuxi**.

The disruption reportedly affected approximately **18 flights** overall.

Contemporary Chinese reporting estimated that **more than 2,000 passengers** were affected.

This is one of the strongest documented aspects of the entire case.

There is no need to rely on UFO websites to establish that the airport disruption occurred.

Contemporary mainstream news organizations reported it, and Chinese aviation authorities acknowledged the operational response.

## **What Did the Flight Crew See?**

The available public record does not provide a complete technical pilot report with precise measurements of the object.

The contemporary accounts describe a **bright or twinkling unidentified object** in the vicinity of the airport.

The object was reportedly seen by flight crews rather than being discovered through radar.

The later Beijing–Shanghai investigation specifically stated that the airport's radar had **not detected the unidentified object** and that the report came from the crews of two flights.

That means the commonly repeated statement that **"airport radar tracked the UFO"** should not be presented as an established fact.

The available investigation evidence points in the opposite direction.

**Object sighting duration ≠ airport closure duration.**

The closure was a safety response, not proof that the object remained continuously visible for an hour.

## **The First Photographs Appear**

While the airport incident was unfolding, photographs taken by Hangzhou residents began circulating online.

One of the most famous photographs was taken by a local resident named **Ma Shijun (麻世俊)**.

Ma had recently purchased a digital SLR camera and was out with his wife.

At approximately **8:26 p.m.**, he noticed a bright light overhead.

He looked up and saw what he described as a bright white streak moving across the sky.

He photographed it.

The resulting image showed a bright elongated object with multiple points of light and became one of the most recognizable images associated with the Hangzhou UFO story.

But there was a crucial problem.

Ma himself did **not** claim that he knew what he had photographed.

In other words, even the photographer did not claim certainty.

## **Ma Shijun's Photograph Was Probably Not the Airport UFO**

This is one of the most important findings in the entire case.

The photograph was taken at **8:26 p.m.**

The airport incident was reported around **8:40 p.m.**

More importantly, the later investigation compared the photograph with the reported airport sighting and concluded that the photographed object was an **aircraft**, not the unidentified object that caused the airport disruption.

The Beijing–Shanghai UFO investigation reported that Ma's photograph showed an aircraft that was **landing**, and that there was no evidence establishing a connection between that aircraft and the airport UFO.

Chinese astronomers had already been skeptical of the photograph.

Beijing Planetarium curator **Zhu Jin** said the image looked like an aircraft with flashing navigation/strobe lights photographed at night.

National Astronomical Observatories researcher **Hu Jingyao** also argued that the photograph showed an ordinary aircraft rather than an extraterrestrial craft.

There was another clue suggesting that Ma's photograph and the airport sighting were different events.

But the apparent height of the object in Ma's photograph did not match the descriptions of the airport sighting.

Chinese investigators therefore concluded that the photograph should not be treated as photographic evidence of the airport UFO.

This is a good example of why timing alone is not enough to connect two UFO photographs.

Two strange lights seen in the same city on the same night do not automatically represent the same object.
