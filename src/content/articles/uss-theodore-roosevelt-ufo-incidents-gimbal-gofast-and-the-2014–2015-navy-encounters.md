# **The USS Theodore Roosevelt UFO Story**

When people talk about the modern U.S. Navy UFO mystery, they often mention the famous **GIMBAL** and **GOFAST** videos.

But those videos were only part of a much larger story.

Between roughly **2014 and 2015**, Navy F/A-18 pilots and other personnel associated with the **USS Theodore Roosevelt Carrier Strike Group** reported a recurring series of unidentified objects during military training along the U.S. East Coast.

The reports included radar contacts, infrared detections, visual observations and encounters that pilots considered unusual enough to become a **flight-safety issue**.

One of the most striking accounts came from former Navy F/A-18 pilot **Ryan Graves**, who described a near-miss involving an object that appeared to him and other pilots as a **dark cube surrounded by a transparent sphere**.

Later, two infrared videos from Navy F/A-18 aircraft became publicly known as **GIMBAL** and **GOFAST**.

The U.S. Department of Defense eventually confirmed that the videos were genuine Navy footage and officially released them in April 2020. The Pentagon said at the time that the phenomena shown remained unidentified.

However, there is an important modern development:

**Being officially unidentified does not mean being extraterrestrial.**

And one of the two famous videos, **GOFAST**, has since received a much more ordinary interpretation from both NASA and the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office.

The broader Roosevelt encounters remain much less completely resolved because the public does not have the underlying radar datasets and complete sensor records needed to reconstruct every reported encounter.

# **What Was the USS Theodore Roosevelt?**

The **USS Theodore Roosevelt (CVN-71)** is a U.S. Navy nuclear-powered aircraft carrier.

Its carrier air wing operates fighter aircraft including the **F/A-18 Super Hornet**.

During the period relevant to this case, Navy aviators were conducting demanding training missions over the Atlantic.

These were not casual flights.

The pilots were operating sophisticated fighter aircraft equipped with advanced radar and infrared targeting systems.

That became important when the pilots began reporting objects that their equipment appeared to detect but that they could not always visually identify.

# **When Did the Encounters Begin?**

Public accounts place the beginning of the broader pattern in **2014**, during Navy training along the U.S. East Coast.

Ryan Graves later said the unusual contacts became noticeable after the F/A-18 fleet received the newer **AN/APG-79 AESA radar**.

The radar upgrade is an important part of the story because it provided the aircraft with substantially improved detection capability compared with the older radar systems.

The pilots initially had no reason to assume they were seeing UFOs.

In fact, the first assumption was much more ordinary:

**radar malfunction.**

Pilots wondered whether the unusual contacts were false radar returns or equipment problems.

According to later accounts, however, the contacts continued.

They were reportedly detected repeatedly enough that the pilots began taking them seriously.

# **The Objects Were Reported Again and Again**

One of the most unusual aspects of the Roosevelt story is its alleged frequency.

Ryan Graves later described the objects as appearing **repeatedly and sometimes almost daily** during the training period.

The reports were not limited to one pilot.

Different aircrew members reportedly encountered or detected unusual objects through different systems.

Some accounts involved:

**Radar contacts**

**Infrared targeting systems**

**Visual observations**

**Objects appearing to remain in the training area**

**Objects apparently moving in unusual ways**

The evidence is not equally strong for every one of these claims.

The public record does not contain the complete raw radar data or a complete roster of every pilot involved.

Therefore, it is more accurate to say that **multiple Navy aviators reported recurring unidentified contacts**, rather than claiming that every pilot saw the same object every day.

# **Ryan Graves and the First Serious Concern**

One of the most important public witnesses is former Navy F/A-18 pilot **Ryan Graves**.

Graves was associated with **Strike Fighter Squadron 11**, the "Red Rippers," during the period.

According to his later account, the pilots initially wondered whether the objects represented a classified American drone or some other secret military technology.

That was actually a reasonable assumption.

The United States was developing increasingly sophisticated unmanned aircraft, and pilots knew that classified programs existed.

But then something happened that changed the way the squadron viewed the problem.

It became a **safety issue**.

# **Why the Near-Miss Mattered**

Military aircraft routinely operate with carefully controlled separation distances.

Two F/A-18s flying in formation already require precise coordination.

An unidentified object suddenly appearing between them could therefore be extremely dangerous.

According to Graves' later account, other pilots had experienced additional near-misses that were not initially reported.

He later said that after the first serious incident, the squadron discovered **four additional near-miss reports** from the previous month.

Because of this, the pilots began treating the objects as aviation hazards rather than simply curiosities.

It is important to label this correctly:

These are **witness accounts**, not a publicly released collection of independent accident-investigation reports.

The underlying safety documentation has not all been made public.

# **The GIMBAL Incident**

One of the most famous episodes occurred in **January 2015**.

A Navy F/A-18 Super Hornet operating with the Roosevelt group recorded infrared footage using its **ATFLIR**, or Advanced Targeting Forward-Looking Infrared system.

The resulting 34-second clip eventually became known as:

**GIMBAL.**

The video shows a bright infrared object against the sky.

The object appears roughly disk-shaped.

Toward the end of the recording, the object appears to rotate.

The pilots can be heard reacting with surprise.

One of the most famous lines from the recording is:

**"Look at that thing!"**

The recording later became one of the most important pieces of evidence in the modern UAP debate.

# **What Does the GIMBAL Video Actually Show?**

The video itself is genuine.

The U.S. Department of Defense officially confirmed that the footage was a Navy video and released it publicly in April 2020.

The object remains officially categorized as **unidentified** in the current AARO imagery library.

But "unidentified" does not mean that the object's appearance proves extraordinary propulsion.

The released video contains only a limited portion of the total information that would be needed for a complete physical reconstruction.

The public does not have:

**The complete raw radar dataset**

**All classified sensor information**

**Every targeting-pod parameter**

**The complete aircrew mission data**

**The full classified intelligence assessment**

Because of this, there are competing interpretations of the apparent rotation.

# **Why Does the GIMBAL Object Appear to Rotate?**

The object appears to rotate relative to the camera.

Some researchers argue that the apparent movement is caused at least partly by the mechanics of the **ATFLIR targeting pod**, including infrared glare and the way the sensor maintains its orientation.

Others argue that the apparent rotation represents genuine movement of the object.

Independent technical studies have examined both interpretations.

A 2023 academic preprint by Yannick Peings and Marik von Rennenkampff argued that, under certain assumptions based on pilot recollections, the object's reconstructed motion could be highly unusual. The paper also discussed a conventional alternative involving infrared glare from a distant jet.

That work is an independent analysis, not a U.S. government determination.

A responsible description is therefore:

**GIMBAL remains unresolved in the public record.**

# **The Famous GIMBAL Audio**

# **The GOFAST Incident**

Another Roosevelt video was recorded in 2015.

It became known as:

**GOFAST.**

The video shows a small bright object apparently moving rapidly across the surface of the ocean.

The name "GOFAST" was given to the video later; it is not the official name of the object.

The clip became famous because the object appears to race across the water at extraordinary speed.

At first glance, it looks like something moving extremely quickly only a short distance above the ocean.

But this is where modern analysis becomes especially important.

# **NASA's Analysis of GOFAST**

NASA's 2023 UAP Independent Study report specifically analyzed the GOFAST video.

NASA calculated that the aircraft was approximately **13,000 feet above the ocean**.

The apparent rapid movement of the object is strongly affected by **parallax** — the apparent motion of a nearby or distant object caused by the movement of the observing aircraft and camera.

Using information displayed in the video, NASA's analysis estimated that the object moved approximately **390 metres over 22 seconds**, producing an average speed of about **40 mph**, although the analysis acknowledged uncertainty because wind effects were not included.

That is dramatically different from the popular claim that GOFAST demonstrates an object travelling at hundreds or thousands of miles per hour just above the ocean.

# **AARO's More Recent GOFAST Analysis**

The Pentagon's **All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)** published a dedicated GOFAST case-resolution report in February 2025.

AARO concluded with **high confidence** that the object did not demonstrate anomalous speed.

Its analysis placed the object at approximately **13,000 feet**.

Depending on its heading relative to the wind, AARO estimated its speed at approximately:

**32–72 metres per second**

or roughly:

**72–161 mph.**

AARO also concluded that the apparent high speed was substantially affected by viewing geometry.

The object itself was not definitively identified, but AARO found **no anomalous performance characteristics**.

This is one of the most important updates to the Roosevelt UFO story.

# **Could GOFAST Have Been a Balloon?**

One proposed interpretation is that GOFAST could represent a balloon or another object drifting with the wind.

The reasoning is straightforward.

If an object is being carried by wind at approximately 13,000 feet, it does not need an engine to move at tens of miles per hour.

NASA noted that the apparent speed could be explained substantially by the aircraft's own motion and parallax, while the object's movement was compatible with ordinary wind speeds.

AARO likewise found no evidence of anomalous propulsion.

However, AARO did not definitively identify the object as a balloon.

So the correct wording is:

**GOFAST has not been definitively identified, but its extraordinary apparent speed is no longer strong evidence for extraordinary propulsion.**

# **The Thermal Appearance of GOFAST**

Another interesting feature is that the object appears bright against a darker ocean background in the infrared image.

NASA noted that the display suggested the object was colder than the ocean rather than showing a strong heat signature from an engine.

The report therefore found no evidence of heat produced by a propulsion system and said the object could plausibly have been drifting with the wind.

Again, this does not identify the object.

It simply makes the most extraordinary interpretations less necessary.

# **The 2019 Navy Reporting Change**

# **What Does NASA Say?**

NASA's 2023 independent UAP study also examined GOFAST.

Its analysis found that the video could create a misleading impression of extraordinary speed because the observing F/A-18 was itself travelling rapidly.

NASA calculated an average object movement of approximately 40 mph over part of the analyzed interval, while noting uncertainty from wind effects.

NASA concluded that the object did not need to be moving at extraordinary speed to produce the appearance seen in the video.

NASA's broader UAP program also emphasizes that the current evidence base is insufficient to draw firm scientific conclusions about the nature of UAP generally.
