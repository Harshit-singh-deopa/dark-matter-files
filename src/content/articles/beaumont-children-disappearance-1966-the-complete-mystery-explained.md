## **Who Were the Beaumont Children?**

The Beaumont children were three siblings from **Somerton Park**, a suburb of Adelaide, South Australia.

They were:

* **Jane Nartare Beaumont — aged 9**
* **Arnna Kathleen Beaumont — aged 7**
* **Grant Ellis Beaumont — aged 4**

Their parents were **Grant "Jim" Beaumont and Nancy Beaumont**.

At the time, Glenelg Beach was a popular destination for Adelaide families, and allowing older children some independence was considerably more common than it is today.

The children's disappearance would eventually become one of Australia's best-known unsolved cases and would profoundly affect public attitudes toward children's safety.

## **Australia Day 1966**

The disappearance occurred on **Wednesday, 26 January 1966**, which was Australia Day.

The Beaumont children lived only a few kilometres from Glenelg Beach.

According to historical accounts, the children wanted to go to Glenelg again after having visited the beach the previous day.

They were considered old enough for the short trip, particularly because Jane was the eldest and could look after her younger brother and sister.

The journey from their home to Glenelg was short.

Historical accounts describe the children travelling by bus to the beach that morning.

## **The Journey to Glenelg**

The children left home in the morning and travelled toward Glenelg.

Contemporary and later investigations established that they reached the Glenelg area.

The important point is that this was not a case where the children disappeared immediately after leaving their house.

There were several reported sightings of them during the day.

This later became one of the most important aspects of the investigation because witnesses were able to provide information about where the children had been and who they may have interacted with.

## **The Last Known Day**

The exact sequence of events is reconstructed from witness statements and police records rather than from a continuous record.

That distinction is important.

There was no camera system recording the children throughout the day, and there is no surviving video showing exactly what happened.

Investigators therefore had to reconstruct the children's movements from people who remembered seeing them.

Several sightings became particularly important.

## **The Children at Glenelg Beach**

The children were seen around the Glenelg beach area.

At the time, Glenelg was busy, particularly because it was Australia Day.

The beach and surrounding areas contained large numbers of people.

Yet despite the crowds, the children eventually disappeared without leaving behind a confirmed trace.

This is one of the most unusual aspects of the case.

They were not missing in an isolated wilderness.

They disappeared in a populated part of Adelaide.

## **The Mysterious Man**

Several witnesses reported seeing the three children with an unidentified man.

Descriptions varied slightly between witnesses, but the man was generally described as **tall, thin-faced, tanned and with short fair or light-coloured hair**.

Police eventually produced a composite sketch based on witness descriptions.

The man became one of the most important figures in the investigation.

However, it is important not to treat the composite sketch as proof that the man committed the crime.

Witness descriptions were made after the event and were subject to the limitations of human memory.

The identity of the man was never conclusively established.

## **The Colley Reserve Sighting**

One of the most important reported sightings occurred around **Colley Reserve**, near Glenelg Beach.

Witnesses reported seeing the children in the company of the unidentified man.

According to the accounts, the children appeared comfortable with him.

This became significant because it suggested that the person was not necessarily someone the children immediately regarded as threatening.

However, exactly who the man was and what happened afterward remains unknown.

South Australian Crime Stoppers lists Colley Reserve as one of the locations where the children's presence was confirmed.

## **The Wenzels Cake Shop Visit**

Another important confirmed sighting occurred at **Wenzels Cake Shop on Mosley Street, Glenelg**.

A shopkeeper remembered Jane purchasing food.

The children's parents had reportedly given them a relatively small amount of money.

The shopkeeper, however, recalled Jane using a **one-pound note** to purchase food, including pasties and a meat pie.

This immediately raised questions.

Where had the one-pound note come from?

Police considered the possibility that the unidentified man had given the children money.

The difference between the money the children were believed to have left home with and the money used at the shop became one of the significant clues in the investigation.

## **The “Pie for the Man” Detail**

Some later accounts state that the children purchased food including a pie for the man.

This detail became part of the popular story of the case.

However, because the historical record is reconstructed from witness memories, it is better to present this as a **witness account rather than an independently verified fact about the man's identity or intentions**.

The more firmly established point is that the children were seen at Wenzels Cake Shop and that the purchase involved money that raised questions for investigators.

## **When Did the Parents Realise Something Was Wrong?**

The historical police record contains an interesting discrepancy concerning the times initially provided by the children's father.

According to an operational log later made public by South Australia Police, Jim Beaumont initially stated that the children had left at about **1:00 p.m.** and were expected home at **5:00 p.m.**

His statement was later amended to indicate that they had left at approximately **10:00 a.m.** and were expected home around **2:00 p.m.**

This discrepancy has sometimes been treated as suspicious in popular discussions.

However, there is no need to interpret it that way.

A police statement made during a rapidly developing missing-person investigation can contain mistakes, misunderstandings or later corrections.

The important historical fact is that the official police log records the discrepancy.

## **The Search Begins**

By the evening, the children had not returned.

Their father reported them missing to Glenelg Police shortly before **7:20 p.m.**, according to the police operational log later released publicly.

Police and family members searched the beach area.

By approximately **8:40 p.m.**, police were also searching the Brighton foreshore, West Beach and Henley Beach.

At approximately **9:50 p.m.**, volunteers from the Sea Rescue Squadron offered to search the coastline.

Police also checked boats and nearby areas.

The investigation expanded rapidly.

## **The First Major Police Search**

The search became one of the largest operations in South Australian history.

Police searched:

* Glenelg Beach
* surrounding streets
* nearby beaches
* drains
* coastal areas
* boats and vessels
* other locations where the children might have travelled

Hundreds of volunteers also became involved.

The scale of the search reflected the enormous public concern surrounding the disappearance.

But despite the effort, the children were not located.

The possibility that this man was responsible for their disappearance became one of the main investigative theories.

Nor has any unidentified man ever been conclusively proven to have abducted the children.

**Several witnesses reported seeing the children with an unidentified man, and police investigated the possibility that this man was connected to their disappearance.**

# **The Abduction Theory**

South Australian authorities currently suspect that the children were **abducted and murdered**.

Crime Stoppers South Australia explicitly states that police suspect this explanation and continues to offer a reward of up to **A$1 million** for information leading to a conviction and/or recovery of the children's remains.

This is the official investigative position.

It is important, however, to distinguish a police theory from a court-established fact.

There has been:

* no conviction

# **The Accidental Drowning Theory**

One of the earliest possibilities considered was that the children may have entered the water and drowned.

# **The Harry Phipps Theory**

One of the most discussed later theories concerns **Harry Phipps**, an Adelaide businessman who owned the former Castalloy factory site in North Plympton.

Phipps was named publicly as a potential person of interest decades after the children's disappearance.

The theory received substantial attention following investigations by journalists and researchers.

It was also connected to claims made by two brothers who said that, as boys, they had been paid to dig a hole at the Castalloy site shortly after the disappearance.

The claims eventually led to multiple searches of the property.
