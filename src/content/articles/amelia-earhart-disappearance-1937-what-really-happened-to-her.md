## **Who Was Amelia Earhart?**

Amelia Mary Earhart was one of the most famous aviators of the early twentieth century.

She was born on **July 24, 1897, in Atchison, Kansas**, and became internationally famous through a series of aviation achievements.

In 1928, she became the first woman to cross the Atlantic by airplane as a passenger. Four years later, in 1932, she became the **first woman to fly solo and nonstop across the Atlantic Ocean**, and only the second person to accomplish the feat after Charles Lindbergh.

She went on to establish several aviation records and became an important public figure in aviation.

By the middle of the 1930s, Earhart had begun planning what would become her most ambitious flight: an attempt to circumnavigate the globe.

The flight would ultimately become the mystery for which she is most remembered.

The Federal Aviation Administration's historical material also records her major aviation achievements and her 1937 attempt to fly around the world.

## **The Plan to Fly Around the World**

Earhart's original around-the-world attempt began in **March 1937**.

She planned a route that would take her generally eastward around the world, but the first attempt ended when the Lockheed Electra crashed during takeoff at Luke Field in Honolulu on **March 20, 1937**.

The aircraft was badly damaged and was sent back to Lockheed in California for extensive repairs.

Earhart eventually restarted the expedition.

This time, she chose an eastbound route.

On **June 1, 1937**, she departed from Miami, Florida, with navigator **Fred Noonan**.

Noonan was an experienced navigator who had worked on long-distance commercial and maritime routes. His job was particularly important because the flight involved enormous stretches of ocean with very few visible landmarks.

Over the following weeks, Earhart and Noonan crossed thousands of miles.

They eventually reached **Lae, New Guinea**, on June 29.

According to the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, they had already traveled approximately **22,000 miles**, with roughly another 7,000 miles remaining before completing the planned journey.

## **The Final Flight: Lae to Howland Island**

The most important part of the entire mystery began on the morning of **July 2, 1937**.

Earhart and Noonan departed Lae, New Guinea, in their Lockheed Model 10-E Electra, registration **NR16020**.

Their destination was **Howland Island**.

The flight was approximately **2,556 miles** over open Pacific Ocean.

Howland was an extremely difficult destination.

The island was tiny—approximately two miles long and less than a mile wide—and surrounded by thousands of miles of ocean.

There were no large cities, mountains or other obvious landmarks nearby that could easily guide an approaching aircraft.

Because of this, radio communication and accurate celestial or dead-reckoning navigation were extremely important.

A U.S. Coast Guard cutter, the **USCGC Itasca**, had been stationed near Howland to assist Earhart.

The ship was supposed to communicate with her aircraft and help guide the Electra toward the island.

The National Archives still preserves the *Itasca* radio logs, communications records, search charts and other material from the operation.

## **The Problem With Radio Communication**

One of the most important facts about the disappearance is that Earhart was actually communicating with the *Itasca*.

However, the communication system did not work as effectively as everyone hoped.

The Smithsonian explains that Earhart and the *Itasca* were unable to establish reliable direct two-way communication.

Neither Earhart nor Noonan was competent in Morse Code, meaning voice radio communication was especially important.

The *Itasca* received multiple transmissions from Earhart.

The problem was that receiving a transmission was not the same thing as being able to have a clear two-way conversation.

This created a potentially serious navigational problem.

The ship could hear Earhart, but she could not always hear or respond to the information the ship was attempting to provide.

The original National Archives radio log records repeated communication difficulties, including problems with reception and attempts to establish contact.

## **The Weather and Flight Conditions**

The final flight also involved difficult environmental conditions.

Earhart and Noonan were flying a very long oceanic route.

The Electra was heavily loaded with fuel, and the flight required extremely accurate navigation.

The destination itself was tiny.

Even a relatively small navigational error could mean flying past Howland Island without seeing it.

## **The Final Radio Communications**

The radio transmissions are among the most important surviving evidence.

The *Itasca* radio log records communications with Earhart's aircraft, which used the call sign **KHAQQ**.

The National Archives notes that Earhart's crew reported difficulties communicating and at one point indicated that they were approximately 200 miles from Howland.

As the morning progressed, the *Itasca* continued attempting to communicate with the aircraft.

At approximately **8:43 a.m. on July 2**, the ship received what is generally regarded as Earhart's final confirmed voice transmission.

The National Air and Space Museum records the message as:

> “We are on the line of position 156-137. Will repeat message. We will repeat this message on 6210 kilocycles. Wait. Listening on 6210 kilocycles. We are running north and south.”

The Smithsonian identifies this as the final transmission recorded from Earhart's aircraft.

The National Archives' surviving radio log provides the primary historical record behind the communications.

After this, no confirmed communication from Earhart's aircraft was received.

## **What Did “156-137” Mean?**

The numbers in Earhart's final transmission have attracted enormous attention.

They are generally interpreted as references to a **line of position**, associated with celestial navigation.

The idea was that Earhart and Noonan were attempting to establish their position and determine where they were relative to Howland Island.

The phrase “running north and south” is particularly important because it suggests that the aircraft was searching along a north-south line for the island.

If they had reached the correct line but could not see Howland, they could potentially have flown north and south looking for it.

But Howland Island was extremely small.

The aircraft could pass within a relatively short distance and still fail to visually locate it.

This is one reason the navigation problem is central to the mystery.
