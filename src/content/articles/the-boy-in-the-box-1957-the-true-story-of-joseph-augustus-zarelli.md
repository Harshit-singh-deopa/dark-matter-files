# **Why Identifying Him Was So Difficult**

The investigation faced an unusual problem.

There was no obvious identification on the child, and investigators could not immediately connect him with a missing-person report.

Police therefore had to start with the most basic question:

**Who was this child?**

The investigation produced a detailed description and photographs, and authorities began distributing information throughout Philadelphia and surrounding areas.

The effort eventually became enormous.

Police circulated thousands of notices to physicians, police departments and members of the public. Later, hundreds of thousands of circulars were distributed with utility bills and displayed in public places, including stores and transportation locations. The Philadelphia Inquirer also publicized the case extensively. ([Inquirer.com](https://www.inquirer.com/news/philadelphia/boy-in-the-box-philadelphia-case-backstory-20221207.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Despite the extraordinary publicity, nobody came forward who could establish the child's identity.

That was one of the most puzzling aspects of the case.

A four-year-old had apparently lived somewhere, had interacted with adults, and had presumably been known to someone. Yet no one successfully identified him.

# **The First Major Theory: The Nearby Foster Home**

One of the longest-running theories involved a foster home located relatively close to where the boy was discovered.

A medical examiner's-office employee named **Remington Bristow** became deeply interested in the case and continued pursuing leads for decades.

According to accounts of the investigation, Bristow became interested in a nearby foster home after receiving information from a psychic.

At an estate sale associated with the property, he reportedly noticed objects that reminded him of evidence from the case, including a bassinet and blankets.

Bristow developed the theory that the boy might have been connected to the household.

This theory became one of the most persistent explanations surrounding the case.

However, resemblance between objects and circumstantial connections did not establish that Joseph had lived there.

Philadelphia investigators later revisited the foster-home theory. By the late 1990s, police investigators and members of the Vidocq Society had interviewed people connected with the household and ultimately closed that line of investigation without establishing a connection between the family and the unidentified boy. ([Kiwix Server](https://kiwi.itspektar.net/content/wikipedia_en_all_maxi/A/Boy_in_the_Box_%28Philadelphia%29?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So while the foster-home theory became an important part of the case's history, it should **not** be presented as a solved explanation.

# **The "Martha" or "M" Theory**

Another major theory appeared in **2002**, when a woman identified publicly only as **"M" or "Martha"** contacted investigators.

She claimed that she knew what had happened to the child and said that her family had acquired a boy several years before his death.

Her account contained details that investigators considered significant enough to examine.

However, the story could not be independently verified.

Investigators found serious problems with the account, including the absence of corroborating evidence connecting the family to the boy.

The story therefore remained a theory rather than an established explanation.

# **Forensic Genetic Genealogy**

The breakthrough was not simply a conventional DNA match.

Investigators used **forensic genetic genealogy**.

Traditional forensic DNA testing generally compares a limited set of genetic markers.
