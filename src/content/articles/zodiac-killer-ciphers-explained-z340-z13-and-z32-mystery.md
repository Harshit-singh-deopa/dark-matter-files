## **The Zodiac Killer and the Beginning of the Cipher Mystery**

The Zodiac Killer was the name adopted by an unidentified person who sent a series of letters and cryptograms to newspapers in Northern California during the late 1960s and early 1970s.

The writer became particularly famous for combining criminal claims with deliberately mysterious communications.

The first major batch of letters arrived on **July 31, 1969**, when letters were sent to the **San Francisco Chronicle, San Francisco Examiner and Vallejo Times-Herald**.

Each newspaper received part of a much larger cipher.

The writer demanded that the newspapers publish the material, threatening further consequences if they did not.

The three sections eventually formed what is now called the **Z408 cipher**.

This was the beginning of one of the most famous cryptographic mysteries in American history.

## **What Made the Zodiac Ciphers Different?**

The Zodiac communications were unusual because they were not simply coded messages.

They combined:

* strange symbols
* letters
* numbers
* repeated characters
* newspaper correspondence
* cryptographic puzzles
* maps
* claims about the writer's crimes
* references to future messages
* challenges directed toward police and the public

The symbols also varied considerably.

Some resembled ordinary alphabetic characters, while others looked like geometric shapes, crosshairs, astronomical symbols and specially constructed marks.

This made the messages visually distinctive and contributed enormously to the Zodiac mystery.

## **The Four Major Zodiac Ciphers**

Four ciphers are usually regarded as the major Zodiac cryptograms:

**Z408 — 408 characters — solved in 1969**

**Z340 — 340 characters — solved in 2020**

**Z13 — 13 characters — still unresolved**

**Z32 — 32 characters — still unresolved**

There are also other Zodiac communications and cards containing unusual symbols or possible hidden messages, but these four are the central cryptographic puzzles discussed by researchers.

## **The Z408 Cipher — The First Major Code**

The first major Zodiac cipher consisted of **408 characters**.

Rather than sending the complete cipher to one newspaper, the writer divided it into three portions.

One portion went to the San Francisco Chronicle.

Another went to the San Francisco Examiner.

The third went to the Vallejo Times-Herald.

The newspapers published the sections, allowing readers to reconstruct the complete message.

This turned the cipher into a public puzzle.

## **Who Solved Z408?**

The solution came surprisingly quickly.

A California schoolteacher, **Donald Gene Harden**, and his wife, **Bettye June Harden**, worked on the cipher after seeing the published material.

They eventually reconstructed the message.

Their solution was essentially a substitution system in which the Zodiac used numerous symbols to represent letters.

The Harden solution was published in newspapers in August 1969.

The decoded message contained the writer's disturbing explanation for why he claimed to kill and included references to his belief that his victims would become his "slaves" after death.

Importantly, however, the message **did not reveal the writer's name**.

That became a recurring pattern throughout the Zodiac cipher mystery.

The first major code was solved, but the identity question remained unanswered.

## **Why Z408 Did Not Solve the Zodiac Mystery**

One of the biggest misconceptions about the Zodiac ciphers is that solving a cipher should automatically reveal the killer.

That did not happen.

The Z408 plaintext contained information about the writer's motives and worldview, but no reliable identification of the person behind the letters.

This meant that even after the first cipher was broken, investigators were still dealing with an unidentified writer.

And then came a much harder challenge.

# **The Z340 Cipher**

On **November 8, 1969**, the Zodiac sent another coded message to the San Francisco Chronicle.

It contained **340 symbols** arranged in a grid.

Researchers eventually called it **Z340** or the **340 Cipher**.

Unlike Z408, this cipher resisted attempts to solve it for decades.

For more than half a century, amateur codebreakers, professional cryptographers, computer scientists and Zodiac researchers attempted to understand its structure.

The message became one of the most famous unsolved cryptograms in the world.

The San Francisco Chronicle's archives contain historical material documenting the correspondence and cipher investigation.

## **Why Was Z340 So Difficult?**

Z340 looked superficially similar to Z408 because it also used repeated symbols and unusual characters.

But the underlying construction was substantially more complicated.

Researchers eventually discovered that the cipher combined **transposition** with a **homophonic substitution system**.

In simple terms, the symbols did not simply correspond directly to letters in a straightforward one-to-one manner.

The arrangement of the characters themselves also mattered.

The way the grid was read had to be understood before the substitution system could be properly interpreted.

That combination helped keep the cipher hidden for 51 years.

The eventual academic paper describing the solution identifies the cipher as a transposition and homophonic substitution system with unusual characteristics.

# **The 51-Year Wait**

For decades, Z340 became the centerpiece of Zodiac cryptography.

Many people claimed to have solved it.

Most proposed solutions failed because they could not demonstrate that the proposed plaintext was uniquely supported by the cipher.

A genuine cryptographic solution needs more than producing English-looking words.

It must explain the entire cipher using a reproducible method.

This distinction became extremely important because the Zodiac's shorter ciphers produced enormous numbers of possible interpretations.

# **The 2020 Z340 Breakthrough**

In December **2020**, an international team finally produced a convincing solution to Z340.

The team consisted of:

* **David Oranchak**, an American computer programmer and cryptography researcher
* **Sam Blake**, an Australian mathematician
* **Jarl Van Eycke**, a Belgian programmer

The solution was subsequently verified by the **FBI**.

The breakthrough ended a 51-year cryptographic mystery.

## **How Z340 Was Solved**

The solution was not simply a matter of replacing symbols with letters.

The researchers discovered that the cipher involved multiple layers.

A major part of the solution involved determining how the characters had been rearranged within the grid.

The plaintext was then recovered using a combination of cryptanalytic techniques, including substitution analysis and computational search.

The researchers used software to explore enormous numbers of possible configurations.

This was a major difference from the original 1969 attempts.

Modern computing allowed researchers to test possibilities at a scale that would have been practically impossible by hand.

The detailed methodology is documented in the researchers' published paper.

# **What Did the Z340 Cipher Say?**

The decoded message begins with the writer addressing people who were trying to catch him.

It includes the famous statement:

> "I hope you are having lots of fun in trying to catch me."

The message also discusses the writer's apparent belief that he would receive some kind of afterlife reward for killing people.

It refers to the gas chamber and contains a statement denying that he was the person who appeared on a television program.

The message ends with a series of unusual statements and misspellings.

Most importantly, **the Z340 solution did not reveal the Zodiac Killer's identity**.

That was one of the biggest disappointments for people who had expected the 51-year-old cipher to finally expose the person behind the Zodiac letters.

# **What the Z340 Solution Proved**

The successful solution demonstrated several important things.

First, the Zodiac really was capable of constructing relatively complicated cryptographic messages.

Second, the apparent randomness of the symbols concealed a structured system.

Third, the writer deliberately designed at least some of his ciphers to resist straightforward analysis.

And finally, solving the code did not necessarily mean solving the identity mystery.

Z340 gave researchers more information about the writer's communications, but it did not provide the definitive name everyone hoped to find.

# **The Z13 Cipher — "My Name Is..."**

The next major mystery is considerably shorter.

It contains only **13 characters**.

It is generally called **Z13** or the **"My Name Is" cipher**.

It appeared in a Zodiac letter dated **April 20, 1970**.

The letter introduced the cipher with wording equivalent to:

**"My name is—"**

That made researchers immediately suspect that the 13 symbols might represent the writer's real name.

If that interpretation is correct, Z13 could potentially be one of the most important pieces of evidence in the entire Zodiac case.

There is one enormous problem.

There are only 13 symbols.

# **Why Z13 Is So Difficult**

Short ciphers are extremely difficult to validate.

Imagine someone gives you thirteen symbols and asks you to determine a person's name.

There are countless ways to construct a mapping that produces a plausible name.

The shorter the message, the fewer repeated patterns there are available to test.

That means a person can sometimes create a proposed "solution" that looks convincing without actually proving that the Zodiac intended it.

This is why researchers generally demand much stronger evidence before accepting a Z13 solution.

The current specialist literature continues to classify Z13 as unsolved.

# **The Biggest Problem With Z13 Solutions**

Suppose someone proposes that Z13 spells a particular person's name.

That alone does not establish that the person was the Zodiac.

A convincing solution would need to explain:

* why each symbol represents the proposed character
* why the cipher's structure supports the interpretation
* why alternative names are rejected
* whether the same cryptographic system appears elsewhere
* whether the solution is reproducible
* whether independent researchers can obtain the same result

Without those tests, almost any short cipher can become a vehicle for confirmation bias.

This is one of the reasons responsible Zodiac research avoids declaring a suspect identified simply because somebody has found a name that fits 13 symbols.
