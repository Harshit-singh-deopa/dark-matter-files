# **What Is the Voynich Manuscript?**

The Voynich Manuscript is an illustrated codex written on **vellum**, a writing material traditionally made from animal skin.

It is preserved at Yale University under the shelfmark **Beinecke MS 408**.

Yale describes it as a manuscript produced in Central Europe toward the end of the 15th century or during the 16th century, while scientific testing of its material supports a 15th-century origin.

The manuscript is famous because its writing system has never been convincingly deciphered.

Unlike a damaged inscription containing only a few unknown symbols, the Voynich Manuscript contains a substantial amount of writing.

That makes the mystery much more interesting.

There appears to be an organized system behind the symbols.

Words and groups of symbols recur.

Different sections have different patterns.

The text is arranged in ways that resemble organized writing.

But nobody has demonstrated a translation that the wider scholarly community accepts.

# **How Old Is the Manuscript?**

One of the most important discoveries about the Voynich Manuscript came from scientific testing of its parchment.

Radiocarbon dating placed the vellum in the **early 15th century, approximately 1404–1438**.

This is extremely important because it rules out the idea that the manuscript itself was created by Wilfrid Voynich in the 20th century.

Voynich did not create the physical manuscript.

He purchased an already-existing medieval codex in 1912.

Yale's own description notes that scientific testing is consistent with manuscripts produced during the 15th century.

The precise date of the writing and illustrations can be more complicated than simply saying “the manuscript was made in 1404.”

Radiocarbon dating establishes an approximate age for the **vellum**, not necessarily the exact date when every line of ink or every illustration was added.

Nevertheless, the material evidence firmly places the manuscript in the medieval period.

# **Where Was It Made?**

The exact birthplace of the manuscript is unknown.

The strongest historical description places its production somewhere in **Central Europe**.

Yale describes the manuscript as having been produced in Central Europe around the end of the 15th or during the 16th century, while the scientific dating points toward the 15th century.

Researchers have proposed various possible regions, including areas associated with:

* modern-day Italy
* Bohemia
* Germany
* Austria
* other parts of Central Europe

But none of these locations has been conclusively established.

Therefore, it is safer to say:

**The manuscript is generally regarded as a Central European work, but its exact place of creation remains unknown.**

# **Who Wrote the Voynich Manuscript?**

Nobody knows.

The manuscript does not contain an accepted author identification.

For decades, researchers naturally assumed that one person might have written it.

However, palaeographic research has produced a much more interesting possibility.

Medieval manuscript scholar **Lisa Fagin Davis** studied the handwriting and concluded that the manuscript appears to have been written by **five different scribes**. Yale highlighted this research in 2024.

If this interpretation is correct, the manuscript was not simply the work of one mysterious individual.

It may have been created by a group.

That raises another question:

**Why would several people collaborate on a manuscript written in a script that nobody today can understand?**

There is currently no definitive answer.

# **What Does the Writing Look Like?**

The manuscript uses a distinctive collection of symbols often informally called **Voynichese**.

The characters have been catalogued and transliterated by researchers so that computers and scholars can analyze them.

Some symbols resemble familiar letters.

Others look completely unfamiliar.

The apparent writing is arranged into words and lines.

Some symbols occur frequently.

Others are rare.

Different parts of the manuscript also appear to use somewhat different writing patterns.

This has caused researchers to debate whether the manuscript represents:

* an unknown natural language
* a cipher
* a constructed language
* a mnemonic system
* an artificial writing system
* several writing systems
* or deliberately generated text

The biggest problem is that statistical patterns alone cannot tell us exactly what the symbols mean.

**Bathing or balneological scenes**

**Pharmaceutical illustrations**

Herbs and roots are shown alongside containers resembling jars or vessels.

**Text-only pages**

Some pages contain mostly writing, with star-like symbols marking individual entries.

Yale describes these as possibly resembling recipes, although the actual meaning remains unknown.

This has encouraged the theory that the manuscript may have been related to medicine or herbal knowledge.

But plausibility is not proof.

Without being able to read the accompanying writing, researchers cannot establish exactly what these pages were intended to communicate.

# **Why Is It Called the Voynich Manuscript?**

The manuscript received its modern name from **Wilfrid M. Voynich**, the Polish-American bookseller who acquired it in 1912.

Voynich became fascinated by the mysterious codex and promoted the idea that it represented an important lost work.

His name eventually became attached to the manuscript itself.

The manuscript's formal Yale designation is **Beinecke MS 408**.

# **The Manuscript Before Voynich**

The history before Voynich is incomplete, but some important clues survive.

The first confirmed owner was **Rudolf II**, Holy Roman Emperor.

According to the historical record preserved with the manuscript, Rudolf II reportedly paid **600 gold ducats** for it.

He also believed that the manuscript might have been written by the famous medieval scholar **Roger Bacon**.

However, the Roger Bacon attribution is now regarded as speculation rather than established fact. Yale explicitly describes the association as unsupported speculation.

# **The Roger Bacon Theory**

Roger Bacon was a 13th-century English philosopher and scholar associated with science, mathematics and natural philosophy.

Because of the manuscript's unusual illustrations and mysterious writing, the idea that Bacon created it was attractive to early researchers.

But the manuscript's scientific dating creates a major problem.

The vellum dates to the early 15th century.

Roger Bacon died in the 13th century.

Therefore, **Roger Bacon could not have physically written the manuscript on the vellum that was tested**.

This does not prevent someone from copying an earlier work by Bacon centuries later, but there is no convincing evidence that this happened.

The Bacon theory should therefore be treated as an old historical hypothesis, not an established fact.

# **Jacobus Horčicky de Tepenec**

The manuscript later became associated with **Jacobus Horčicky de Tepenec**, a pharmacist and figure connected with the imperial court.

His name appears in historical evidence associated with the manuscript.

This is important because it provides one of the links between the manuscript and the intellectual environment of Central Europe.

However, ownership does not necessarily tell us who created the manuscript.

# **Georgius Barschius**

The manuscript later came into the possession of **Georgius Barschius**, an alchemist and scholar.

Barschius was interested in the mysterious text and apparently sought assistance in understanding it.

His ownership provides another important link in the manuscript's history.

# **Johannes Marcus Marci**

After Barschius, the manuscript came into the possession of **Johannes Marcus Marci of Cronland**.

Marci is particularly important because he sent the manuscript to the famous Jesuit scholar **Athanasius Kircher** in 1666.

He included a letter with the manuscript.

That letter remains an important historical document.

Yale's provenance record confirms this chain of ownership.

# **Athanasius Kircher**

Athanasius Kircher was one of the most famous scholars of the 17th century.

He studied languages, natural philosophy, mathematics, archaeology, religion and many other subjects.

Marci hoped that Kircher might be able to decipher the manuscript.

Kircher apparently never produced a successful translation.

The manuscript then largely disappeared from mainstream scholarly attention for centuries.

# **The Jesuit Connection**

Eventually the manuscript entered the Jesuit collection in Rome.

In 1912, Wilfrid Voynich acquired it from the Jesuit College at **Frascati near Rome**.

The exact details of the surrounding Jesuit manuscript sales have been investigated extensively by later researchers.

Yale's current provenance record states that Voynich purchased the manuscript from the Jesuit College at Frascati in 1912.

# **Wilfrid Voynich's Discovery**

Voynich was fascinated by rare books and manuscripts.

After obtaining the mysterious codex, he began investigating its history.

He believed it was an important work and tried to identify its author and meaning.

He also promoted it to scholars and cryptographers.

His discovery transformed the manuscript from an obscure Renaissance codex into one of the world's most famous cryptographic mysteries.

# **The Manuscript Reaches Yale**

After Wilfrid Voynich died, ownership passed through his wife **Ethel Voynich** and later **Anne Nill**.

Nill eventually sold the manuscript to New York bookseller **H. P. Kraus** in 1961.

Kraus subsequently gave it to Yale University's Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library in **1969**.

It remains there today as **Beinecke MS 408**.

# **The Manuscript Is Real**

This point is extremely important.

The Voynich Manuscript is not merely an internet legend.

It is a real physical medieval manuscript.

Its parchment has undergone scientific examination.

Its pigments and inks have also been analyzed.

Yale's published chemical analysis identified materials including **iron-gall ink, azurite, red ochre and other pigment components** in sampled areas.

The physical manuscript therefore provides a substantial amount of evidence that can be studied independently of theories about its meaning.

# **Does the Manuscript Contain a Fake Language?**

However, the manuscript's statistical structure has made this debate complicated.

Researchers have found patterns in the text that resemble some properties found in natural languages.

That does not prove it is a natural language.

But it does make the question more interesting.

# **The Hoax Theory**

One possibility is that the manuscript was created as an elaborate deception.

# **The Natural Language Theory**

Attempts to match it with languages such as Latin, Hebrew, Romance languages and others have produced interesting proposals but no universally accepted solution.

# **The Cipher Theory**

# **The Famous Cipher Attempts**

Over the past century, cryptographers, linguists, historians and computer scientists have attempted to solve the manuscript.

Several proposals have received significant public attention.

But none has achieved the standard required for a generally accepted decipherment.

A convincing solution would need to explain a large portion of the manuscript consistently, produce meaningful translations and allow independent researchers to reproduce the results.

# **William Newbold's Theory**

One early attempt came from **William Newbold**, a professor who believed the manuscript contained microscopic encoded symbols.

He proposed that Roger Bacon had written hidden scientific information into the manuscript.

His interpretation attracted attention but was later rejected.

Newbold's work became an early example of a recurring problem in Voynich research:

Once researchers expect to see hidden patterns, they can sometimes find patterns that are actually artifacts of the method they are using.

# **Joseph Martin Feely**

Other early researchers attempted to interpret the symbols as encrypted Latin.

These approaches produced proposed readings but failed to produce a consistent translation of the entire manuscript.

The same basic problem has affected many later attempts.

# **The Prescott Currier Analysis**

In the 20th century, researcher **Prescott Currier** performed important statistical and linguistic analysis.

Currier observed that the manuscript seemed to contain different writing styles or “languages.”

He identified what became known as **Currier A and Currier B**.

These observations remain important because they suggest that the manuscript may not have been written as one completely uniform system.

Later palaeographic work suggesting multiple scribes added another layer to the question.

# **Stephen Bax's Proposal**

Linguist **Stephen Bax** proposed a partial decipherment in 2014.

He suggested possible readings for certain words and illustrations.

His approach was deliberately cautious compared with some later claims.

However, his proposal did not become an accepted complete decipherment.

It remains an interesting example of the continuing effort to understand the manuscript.

# **The Hebrew Cipher Proposal**

In 2018, computer scientists **Greg Kondrak and Bradley Hauer** used computational language-analysis techniques to suggest that the manuscript could involve encrypted Hebrew.

Their work attracted considerable media attention.

But it was not a complete decipherment.

Kondrak himself treated the result as a hypothesis rather than a definitive translation.

The proposal therefore did not solve the manuscript.

# **The Gerard Cheshire Controversy**

In 2019, researcher **Gerard Cheshire** claimed that the manuscript represented a form of proto-Romance language.

The claim attracted major headlines.

However, the University of Bristol later withdrew its associated press release, and specialists criticized the methodology.

The proposal is not accepted as the solution to the manuscript.

This episode demonstrates why sensational headlines about the Voynich Manuscript should be treated cautiously.

# **Nicholas Gibbs' Theory**

Writer and researcher **Nicholas Gibbs** proposed that the manuscript was connected to medieval medical knowledge and abbreviated Latin.

His theory received significant attention.

But specialists questioned the proposed readings and the manuscript's supposed structure.

It did not produce a generally accepted translation.
