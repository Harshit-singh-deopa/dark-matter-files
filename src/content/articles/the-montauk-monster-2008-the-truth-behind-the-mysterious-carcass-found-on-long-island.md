# **The Montauk Monster**

**Location:** Ditch Plains Beach, Montauk, Long Island, New York, USA
**Date of discovery:** July 12, 2008

# **The Discovery at Ditch Plains**

According to the contemporary accounts, the story began on **July 12, 2008**, when 26-year-old Montauk resident **Jenna Hewitt** and three friends encountered a strange carcass on **Ditch Plains Beach**, roughly two miles east of Montauk's main business district.

The group reportedly included Hewitt, Rachel Goldberg and Courtney Fruin.

Hewitt photographed the carcass.

At the time, the people who encountered it did not know what animal they were looking at. Its appearance was unusual enough that the photograph became the most important piece of evidence associated with the case.

The East Hampton Star later reported that the carcass had been found near the Surfside restaurant area.

The precise circumstances surrounding the carcass's arrival on the beach were never established.

# **The Photograph That Started the Mystery**

The photograph showed what appeared to be a four-legged animal lying on the beach.

Several features immediately attracted attention.

The animal appeared to have:

* A relatively large, rounded body
* Thin-looking limbs
* Small clawed front paws
* Very little visible hair
* An unusual head and snout
* Visible teeth
* A long-looking tail
* A strange, almost beak-like upper facial structure

The lack of a recognizable face was particularly important.

To someone unfamiliar with animal decomposition, the exposed structures could easily appear to belong to a completely different animal.

The first photograph circulated locally before spreading rapidly through blogs, email and online news sites.

Later photographs reportedly showed the carcass from additional angles. These images became important because they provided more information about the animal's paws, teeth, body and skull.

Palaeozoologist **Darren Naish**, who later examined the photographs, argued that the additional views made the raccoon explanation substantially stronger.

# **The First Newspaper Story**

The case first received local newspaper attention through **The Independent**, a Montauk-area publication.

On **July 23, 2008**, the newspaper published a story titled **“The Hound of Bonacville.”**

The title was a playful reference to both the local term **“Bonackers”** and Arthur Conan Doyle's famous story *The Hound of the Baskervilles*.

The newspaper discussed several possibilities for the animal, including the possibility of a turtle or an unusual animal associated with the nearby Plum Island facility.

Importantly, the article also reported that **Larry Penny**, then East Hampton's Natural Resources Director, believed the carcass was a **raccoon with its upper jaw missing**.

At this stage, however, the story had not yet become the worldwide internet phenomenon that it would soon become.

# **How the Name “Montauk Monster” Appeared**

The phrase **“Montauk Monster”** became widely associated with the case after cryptozoologist **Loren Coleman** published about the photograph on **July 29, 2008**, through his website Cryptomundo.

Coleman titled his article **“Cryptid Washes Ashore At Montauk.”**

He questioned what the animal could be and highlighted the strange appearance of the photograph. He also noted that there was no obvious object in the image that could establish the carcass's scale.

The nickname spread rapidly.

Around the same time, the photograph was being discussed by Gawker and other websites, causing the image to move far beyond Montauk.

This was the period when blogs and online forums could turn an unusual local story into a national internet phenomenon within hours.

# **The Internet Takes Over**

By late July 2008, the photograph had appeared on numerous websites.

People began examining the image, enlarging it, comparing it with animal photographs and suggesting possible explanations.

The story became particularly attractive to the internet because the evidence was limited to photographs and eyewitness accounts.

There was:

* No preserved carcass
* No complete skeleton
* No DNA sample
* No controlled scientific examination
* No official species identification based on laboratory testing

That combination created a large space for speculation.

The mystery became bigger than the physical evidence itself.

# **What Did People Think It Was?**

Several explanations circulated during the first few days.

Some were reasonable biological possibilities.

Others were speculative.

And a few became part of the mythology surrounding the case.

## **A Raccoon**

The raccoon explanation became one of the strongest conventional interpretations.

Larry Penny, the East Hampton Natural Resources Director, reportedly identified the animal as a raccoon.

Darren Naish later independently examined the photographs and reached a similar conclusion.

His reasoning focused on anatomy rather than the creature's overall appearance.

He pointed to the animal's:

* Dentition
* Skull structure
* Front paws
* Claws
* Body proportions
* Tail
* Overall carnivoran anatomy

Naish argued that the apparent “beak” was not evidence of an unknown animal. Instead, it could represent the exposed bones of the snout after soft tissue had decomposed.

He also explained that raccoons have surprisingly dexterous front paws, which fit the unusual-looking hands visible in the photographs.

# **Why Would a Raccoon Look So Strange?**

This is probably the most important part of the case.

A dead mammal does not necessarily continue to resemble the living animal.

When a carcass remains in water and is exposed to waves, microorganisms and the environment, soft tissues can disappear at different rates.

Hair can be lost.

Skin can separate.

Facial tissue can disappear.

The nose and lips can become absent.

Parts of the skull can become exposed.

The result can look dramatically different from the original animal.

Darren Naish specifically described the Montauk carcass as an example of **taphonomy**—the physical and biological processes that affect an organism after death.

He compared the apparent “beak” to exposed facial bones rather than a genuine beak.

This explanation also helps explain why the animal initially looked almost impossible to identify.

# **The Dog or Coyote Theory**

Another serious possibility was that the animal was a **dog or coyote**.

The body shape was sufficiently dog-like that several observers considered this explanation.

William Wise of Stony Brook University's Living Marine Resources Institute examined the photographs.

According to accounts of his assessment, Wise was skeptical that the image represented a genuine unknown animal and considered a diseased or water-degraded dog or coyote as a possibility.

However, he also noted anatomical inconsistencies with a typical canine.

The head and paws did not provide as convincing a match as the raccoon interpretation.

So while the dog/coyote theory cannot simply be declared impossible from the photograph alone, it became less persuasive when the animal's detailed anatomy was considered.

# **The Turtle Theory**

One of the earliest suggestions was that the carcass might be a **sea turtle without its shell**.

The idea gained attention because the body looked unusual and the head seemed vaguely turtle-like.

But this explanation has a major biological problem.

A turtle's shell is not simply an external object that can fall off.

The shell is structurally integrated with the animal's skeleton.

Furthermore, the visible teeth in the photograph did not fit a modern sea turtle.

Loren Coleman noted this problem in his original Cryptomundo discussion.

The turtle explanation therefore became much less credible once basic anatomy was considered.

# **The Large Rodent Theory**

# **Could It Have Been an Unknown Species?**

This was the question that made the Montauk Monster famous.

A small number of people considered the possibility that the carcass represented an unknown animal.

From a scientific perspective, however, there is an important distinction:

**An unidentified photograph is not the same thing as evidence of an unknown species.**

A new species would normally require physical evidence that could be studied.

Scientists would want anatomical material, genetic material, skeletal characteristics or other reproducible evidence.

In the Montauk case, the complete carcass was not preserved for scientific examination.

Therefore, there is no scientific evidence establishing that the animal represented a previously unknown species.

# **Why Decomposition Is So Important**

The Montauk case demonstrates a phenomenon that is common in natural-history mysteries.

Humans are very good at recognizing familiar animals when they are alive.

We are much less reliable when looking at a severely altered carcass.

A raccoon normally has:

* Dense fur
* A recognizable facial mask
* A compact face
* A familiar body shape
* Clearly recognizable paws

Remove much of the fur and soft tissue, expose parts of the skull and allow the body to become distorted by water, and the visual impression changes dramatically.

This is why the Montauk Monster looked so strange despite having anatomy that could be consistent with a familiar North American mammal.
