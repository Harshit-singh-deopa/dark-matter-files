# **Where Is the Amityville Horror House?**

The house associated with the case is in **Amityville, Suffolk County, Long Island, New York**.

The property was publicly known as **112 Ocean Avenue** during the events. Its address was later changed to **108 Ocean Avenue**, partly to reduce unwanted attention from tourists and paranormal enthusiasts. Modern photographs therefore may show a different address from the one appearing in historical accounts.

The house itself was not an ancient building. It was constructed in the late 1920s, decades before the events that made it famous. Skeptical investigators have also challenged later claims that the property had an established history of witchcraft or supernatural activity.

The Society for Psychical Research has noted that some of the more elaborate historical claims surrounding the property are difficult to verify. In particular, claims connecting the property to a supposed witch named John Ketcham have not been firmly established.

# **The DeFeo Family Moves Into the House**

Before the Amityville Horror became associated with ghosts, the house was simply a family home.

Ronald and Louise DeFeo moved into the property in the 1960s with their children. The family lived there for several years.

Their oldest son, **Ronald DeFeo Jr., commonly known as "Butch,"** was 23 years old in 1974.

Nothing about the property's documented history before November 1974 establishes that it was a haunted house.

That distinction is important because later versions of the Amityville story sometimes present the property as though it had been known for supernatural activity for generations. The historical evidence for such a long-running haunting is considerably weaker than the later legend suggests.

# **The November 13, 1974 Murders**

The event that unquestionably occurred at the house happened on **November 13, 1974**.

Six members of the DeFeo family were killed inside the home:

* Ronald DeFeo Sr.
* Louise DeFeo
* Dawn DeFeo
* Allison DeFeo
* Marc DeFeo
* John Matthew DeFeo

Ronald DeFeo Jr. was subsequently arrested and charged.

The case attracted enormous attention because the victims were members of the same family and the crime occurred in what had been an ordinary suburban neighborhood.

Investigators also faced questions about how the killings could have happened without the other occupants of the house apparently responding to the sound. Those circumstances became part of the later public fascination with the case.

The murders themselves, however, should not be confused with the later paranormal story. **There is no evidence that investigators at the time concluded that supernatural activity caused the deaths.**

# **Ronald DeFeo Jr.'s Arrest and Trial**

DeFeo initially gave investigators different explanations for what had happened.

He was eventually prosecuted for the deaths.

His trial began in **October 1975**. His defense argued that he suffered from serious mental problems and attempted to establish an insanity defense.

The court did not accept that defense.

In November 1975, DeFeo was found guilty of **six counts of second-degree murder** and was subsequently sentenced to six consecutive terms of 25 years to life in prison.

DeFeo remained imprisoned for decades.

He died in prison on **March 12, 2021**, aged 69.

His later statements about the murders changed over time, but none of those later claims established a supernatural explanation for what happened.

# **The House Goes on the Market**

After the murders and DeFeo's conviction, the property became difficult to sell.

The house had acquired a disturbing reputation because of the crime.

Then, in **December 1975**, George and Kathy Lutz purchased it.

George Lutz was a businessman and surveyor. Kathy was his wife, and they had three children.

The family reportedly viewed the property primarily as a large house that could provide them with the space and facilities they wanted.

According to later accounts, they were aware of the murders before purchasing it.

This point is significant: **the Lutz family did not move into an unknown house and only later discover its criminal history. They knew about the DeFeo murders.**

# **The Lutz Family Moves In**

The Lutz family moved into the house in December 1975.

The exact date has been reported differently in different accounts, although December 1975 is firmly established.

According to the family's version of events, unusual experiences began almost immediately.

The family remained in the house for approximately **28 days**.

By January 1976, they had left.

The short duration of their stay became one of the defining elements of the Amityville story.

# **What Did the Lutz Family Claim Happened?**

The events described in Jay Anson's 1977 book are extensive.

According to the Lutz family's account, they experienced a progressively disturbing series of incidents.

These reportedly included:

* unusual smells

* cold areas within the house

Some accounts also described much more dramatic events, including alleged levitation, green or black substances appearing inside the house, strange footprints and an unusual creature or entity associated with the family.

These details became famous largely through **Jay Anson's 1977 book**, rather than through independently verified police or scientific records.

That distinction is extremely important.

The book is a record of the story that the Lutz family told. It is **not independent proof that every event described actually occurred**.

# **The Famous 28 Days**

The Lutz family's stay lasted approximately 28 days.

According to their account, the situation became progressively worse.

George reportedly became increasingly preoccupied with the house and its problems. Kathy described frightening experiences involving the children and the home's interior.

The family eventually decided to leave.

They left many of their possessions behind, according to later accounts, and moved away.

The family's departure transformed what might otherwise have remained a private story into a major paranormal controversy.

# **Did the Police Investigate the Haunting?**

This is one of the most important questions surrounding the case.

The popular version of the story can give the impression that police repeatedly witnessed supernatural activity inside the house.

The historical record is much less dramatic.

Skeptical investigators have pointed out that there was no independent police documentation establishing the paranormal events described in the book.

The ABC Science Archive, summarizing skeptical investigations, noted that police records did not show the Lutz family repeatedly calling police for assistance during the supposed haunting period.

That does not prove that the family deliberately invented everything.

It does, however, mean that the strongest evidence for the supernatural claims comes from the people who experienced or reported them rather than from independent investigators.

# **The Book That Made Amityville Famous**

The story became nationally famous through **Jay Anson's 1977 book, *The Amityville Horror: A True Story*.**

Anson did not personally live in the house during the 28-day period.

The book was based primarily on the Lutz family's account and recorded material provided to him.

The book was marketed as a true story and became a major commercial success.

It transformed the house from a local crime scene into one of the world's best-known alleged haunted houses.

The enormous popularity of the book also introduced an important question:

**Was the book a factual account, a dramatized version of real experiences, or an intentionally created story?**

That question has never disappeared.

# **The First Major Controversy**

The Amityville story was controversial almost from the beginning.

Different accounts of what supposedly happened contained inconsistencies.

Some details changed between early newspaper and magazine accounts, the book, later interviews and the movie.

Skeptical investigators began comparing the different versions.

A 1978 investigation published in *The Skeptical Inquirer* identified inconsistencies, exaggerations and factual problems in the book's narrative, including discrepancies concerning dates, weather and other details.

This became one of the earliest serious skeptical challenges to the story.

# **The William Weber Controversy**

One of the most important developments came from **William Weber**, the attorney who had represented Ronald DeFeo Jr.

In 1979, Weber publicly claimed that the Lutz family and he had discussed creating a story about supernatural events.

He said the story had been developed during conversations that included the possibility of turning the events into a book.

Weber later described the story as having been created "over many bottles of wine."

This became one of the strongest arguments used by skeptics to challenge the haunting.

However, Weber's statement should also be treated carefully.

He had become involved in legal and financial disputes connected with the Lutzes, which gave him his own potential motivations and interests.

Therefore, Weber's accusation is **important evidence in the controversy, but it is not by itself proof that every part of the haunting story was fabricated**.

The safest historical conclusion is that Weber claimed the story was constructed, while the Lutz family denied that characterization.

# **The Lutz Family's Position**

George and Kathy Lutz consistently maintained that their experiences were genuine.

They rejected the idea that they had simply invented the haunting to make money.

Their supporters argued that the family's willingness to undergo interviews and even participate in lie-detector testing supported their sincerity.

But sincerity and objective proof are two different things.

A person can genuinely believe that something unexplained happened without the event necessarily having a supernatural cause.

This distinction is important when evaluating the Amityville case.

# **The 1977 Lawsuit**

The controversy eventually moved into the courts.

In 1977, George and Kathy Lutz filed legal action involving William Weber, writer Paul Hoffman and others connected with accounts of the Amityville story.

The dispute involved issues including the use of the Lutz family's names and the publication of competing versions of their story.

The legal conflict became an important part of the Amityville controversy.

By 1979, the litigation had helped expose disagreements about how the story had been developed and who controlled the rights to it.

Contemporary reporting described the situation as a complicated network of lawsuits involving the Lutz family, writers, publishers and others connected with the story.

The litigation does not prove that the haunting was fake.

It does, however, demonstrate that **commercial interests became deeply intertwined with the story very early in its history.**

# **The Warren Investigation**

Another famous chapter involved paranormal investigators **Ed and Lorraine Warren**.

The Warrens became associated with the Amityville case after the Lutz family had left the house.

Their involvement helped bring the story into the wider paranormal community.

According to accounts of the investigation, paranormal researchers visited the property and conducted experiments and séances.

The Warrens concluded that the house contained a powerful supernatural presence.

One famous photograph associated with the investigation was claimed to show the figure of a child.

Supporters of the haunting have regarded the photograph as significant.

Skeptics have questioned both its interpretation and the circumstances under which it was produced.

There is no scientific consensus that the photograph demonstrates the existence of a ghost.

# **What Happened to the House After the Lutzes?**

The Lutz family's departure did not end the story.

Other people subsequently lived in the house.

One of the most important skeptical observations was that later residents did not publicly report the same extraordinary series of supernatural events described by the Lutz family.

The Cromarty family, who later owned the house, became outspoken critics of the haunting story.

According to investigator Joe Nickell's account in *Skeptical Inquirer*, Barbara Cromarty told him that her family experienced no supernatural activity and believed the earlier story was a hoax. She also pointed to physical details of the house that appeared inconsistent with some of the dramatic claims made about damage to doors and windows.

Again, this does not mathematically prove that nothing unusual ever happened.

It does make the case considerably weaker as evidence for a persistent supernatural phenomenon.

# **The Weather Problem**

One particularly interesting skeptical argument concerns the weather described in the book.

Some scenes in *The Amityville Horror* describe severe winter weather, including intense wind, rain, sleet and very low temperatures.

Researchers later compared these descriptions with historical weather records.

A skeptical analysis found discrepancies between the weather described in the book and recorded conditions around the relevant dates.

This does not prove that the Lutz family invented everything.

Weather can vary locally.

However, when a supposedly factual book describes weather as an important part of the story, significant differences between the narrative and historical records become relevant evidence when assessing its reliability.

# **The Alleged Curse of the Property**

# **Why Did the Story Become So Famous?**

Several factors came together.

First, there was a real and disturbing crime.

Second, the next family to live in the house reported an extraordinary sequence of paranormal experiences.

Third, a bestselling book presented those experiences as a true story.

Fourth, paranormal investigators publicly supported the claims.

Finally, Hollywood turned the story into a successful horror film.

The result was a powerful mixture of:

**true crime + alleged paranormal activity + bestselling literature + media coverage + Hollywood.**

That combination made Amityville much larger than the original events themselves.

# **The 1979 Movie**

The first major film adaptation, *The Amityville Horror*, was released in **1979**.

It was inspired by Jay Anson's book rather than being a documentary reconstruction of the actual events.

The movie dramatically increased the international popularity of the Amityville story.

The film also introduced or intensified visual elements that became permanently associated with the legend.

# **What Were the Main Theories?**

The Amityville case has produced several competing explanations.

## **The Genuine Paranormal Theory**

The simplest supernatural explanation is that the Lutz family genuinely experienced paranormal activity.

Supporters point to:

* the family's consistent belief in their experiences
* their reports of multiple types of phenomena
* the involvement of paranormal investigators
* alleged photographs
* accounts from people associated with the investigation

From this perspective, the house may have contained some kind of unexplained phenomenon.

The major problem is the lack of independently verified physical evidence.

No accepted scientific investigation has established that a supernatural entity was present in the house.

## **The Hoax Theory**

The strongest skeptical explanation is that the haunting was largely or entirely fabricated.

Supporters of this theory point to:

* inconsistencies between different accounts
* commercial incentives
* the enormous success of the book
* William Weber's allegations
* the absence of strong independent evidence
* later owners reporting ordinary experiences
* problems identified by skeptical investigators

This is currently one of the most widely accepted explanations among skeptical researchers.

However, saying "the entire story has been scientifically proven to be a hoax" would go too far.

The evidence supports serious doubts, but it cannot reconstruct every private conversation or determine exactly what every member of the Lutz family believed.

## **The Exaggeration Theory**

Another possibility is that something unusual happened but the story became dramatically exaggerated.

Under this explanation:

* the family may have experienced ordinary unexplained events
* fear may have influenced their interpretation
* memories may have changed
* later interviews may have amplified details
* the book may have dramatized events
* paranormal investigators may have interpreted ambiguous events differently

This explanation sits between "everything was supernatural" and "everything was deliberately invented."

It is also difficult to test because there is no independent recording of the alleged events.

## **The Psychological Explanation**

Some skeptics believe psychological factors may have played an important role.

The family knew that six people had recently died in the house.

That knowledge alone could create an intense atmosphere.

Stress, fear, sleep disruption, expectation and heightened attention can influence how ordinary sounds, shadows, smells and household problems are perceived.

This does not mean the Lutz family was lying.

It simply provides a possible explanation for experiences that felt completely real to the people experiencing them.

## **The Environmental Explanation**

Some reported phenomena could potentially have ordinary environmental explanations.

Examples include:

* drafts
* plumbing noises
* heating-system sounds
* structural movement
* insects
* mold or unusual odors
* temperature differences
* electrical problems
* settling materials
* sleep-related experiences

None of these explanations individually accounts for every claim in the story.

They demonstrate, however, why an unusual experience does not automatically require a supernatural explanation.

## **The Curse or Witchcraft Theory**

Another popular theory claims that the property was historically connected to witches or cursed land.

This theory became an important part of the later Amityville mythology.

However, historical research has not established the necessary connection between the property and the supposed witchcraft history.

The John Ketcham connection in particular remains controversial and unsupported by strong evidence.

## **The "Evil Attached to the House" Theory**

Some paranormal interpretations propose that the 1974 murders somehow created or attracted a supernatural presence.

Others suggest that the phenomenon existed before the murders.

There is no reliable evidence establishing either idea.

The murders are documented.

A supernatural connection between the murders and later events is not.

The property continued to be privately owned after the Lutz family left.

# **The Biggest Mystery**

The biggest unresolved question is not necessarily:

**"Was the house haunted?"**

A more historically useful question is:

**"Exactly how did the story of the Amityville haunting develop?"**

We know that:

* a real crime occurred
* the Lutz family subsequently lived there
* they reported extraordinary experiences
* those experiences became commercially successful
* competing versions appeared
* skeptics found significant problems
* supporters continued to believe the family
* no scientific evidence has established a supernatural cause

What remains uncertain is exactly how much of the reported experience was genuine personal experience, misunderstanding, embellishment, deliberate invention, later dramatization, or some combination of these factors.

# **Final Conclusion**

The **Amityville Horror is not one single mystery but two connected historical stories**.

The first is a tragic and well-documented 1974 crime involving six members of the DeFeo family.

The second is the much more controversial story of George and Kathy Lutz and their children, who said they experienced frightening paranormal events after moving into the same house in December 1975.

Their approximately 28-day stay became the foundation for Jay Anson's bestselling 1977 book and the worldwide Amityville legend.

There is **no reliable scientific evidence proving that the house was haunted**. At the same time, it is difficult to reconstruct every private experience or belief of the Lutz family decades later.

The strongest evidence available today supports treating the supernatural portion as an **unverified and heavily disputed account**, rather than established fact.

That distinction is important—not only for historical accuracy, but also because the real tragedy behind the legend involved actual people and families.
