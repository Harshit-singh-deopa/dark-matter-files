# The Jack Family Disappearance: What Happened to Ronald, Doreen, Russell and Ryan?

## **Case Overview**

The **Jack family disappearance** is one of Canada's most haunting unsolved missing-person cases.

On the night of **August 1, 1989**, Ronald “Ronnie” Jack, 26, was at the First Litre Pub in **Prince George, British Columbia** when he reportedly met an unidentified man who offered him temporary work at a logging camp or ranch in the **Cluculz Lake area**, approximately 40 kilometres west of Prince George.

The offer reportedly included work for Ronald, a kitchen job for his partner **Doreen Jack**, and childcare for their two young sons, **Russell, 9, and Ryan, 4**.

The family did not own a vehicle, so the unidentified man offered to drive them to the supposed job site.

In the early hours of **August 2, 1989**, Ronald, Doreen and their two children were seen leaving their home on Strathcona Avenue and getting into the man's dark-coloured four-wheel-drive pickup.

They were never seen again.

More than 35 years later, **Ronald, Doreen, Russell and Ryan Jack remain missing**.

The RCMP investigation remains open. The latest public RCMP update I found, dated August 26, 2024, states that investigators continue to follow up on verifiable tips and that the case will remain open until the family is located.

# **The Four Missing Family Members**

### **Ronald “Ronnie” Jack**

* Age when he disappeared: **26**
* Born: **March 29, 1963**
* Indigenous member of the **Cheslatta Carrier Nation**
* He had previously worked at a sawmill but had suffered a back injury.
* He was unemployed at the time of the disappearance and was looking for work.

### **Doreen Ann Jack**

* Age when she disappeared: **26**
* Born: **April 24, 1963**
* Partner of Ronald Jack
* Indigenous member of the Cheslatta Carrier Nation

### **Russell Fabian Jack**

* Age when he disappeared: **9**
* Born: **February 28, 1980**
* Ronald's stepson/son within the family
* He was expected to return to school in September 1989.

### **Ryan Paul Jack**

* Age when he disappeared: **4**
* Born: **July 26, 1985**
* Youngest member of the family

The four disappeared together and have never been located.

# **The Jack Family's Life Before the Disappearance**

The Jack family had connections to the **Cheslatta Carrier Nation** near Burns Lake, British Columbia.

Before moving to Prince George, the family had been living in the Burns Lake region.

Ronald had worked at a sawmill but suffered a back injury that affected his ability to work.

The family eventually moved to Prince George, hoping Ronald could find employment.

By 1989, the family was experiencing financial difficulties and was dependent on welfare.

This financial situation is important because it may explain why the mysterious job offer was so attractive to Ronald.

The family was not leaving Prince George because they had suddenly decided to disappear.

According to accounts of the case, Ronald told relatives that the work was expected to last only around **10 days to two weeks**, and the family intended to return.

The fact that the family's belongings and the children's school records were left behind later became an important part of the investigation.

# **August 1, 1989 — The Mysterious Job Offer**

On Tuesday evening, **August 1, 1989**, Ronald went to the **First Litre Pub** in Prince George.

While there, he encountered an unidentified man.

The man's offer sounded unusually convenient.

He reportedly told Ronald about employment at a logging camp or ranch somewhere in the **Cluculz Lake area**, west of Prince George.

The reported arrangement was:

* Ronald would work **bucking logs**.
* Doreen would work as a **cook's helper** in the camp kitchen.
* The children could reportedly be cared for at a **daycare facility** at the camp.
* The work would last approximately **10 days to two weeks**.
* Because the Jack family had no vehicle, the man offered to **drive them there**.

The location was believed to be around **Cluculz Lake**, near Highway 16.

The supposed job site was never conclusively established.

This is one of the central mysteries of the case.

**Did the logging camp or ranch actually exist?**

Investigators reportedly checked possible logging operations in the area, but no confirmed job site connected to the family was ever established.

# **Who Was the Mysterious Man?**

Two witness accounts were eventually used to create a description of the unidentified man.

According to case summaries, he was described as:

* A white/Caucasian male
* Approximately **35–40 years old** in 1989

Despite the description and years of investigation, the man's identity has never been publicly established.

# **The Telephone Calls**

The telephone calls made by Ronald shortly before the disappearance are some of the most important pieces of the timeline.

At approximately **11:16 p.m. on August 1**, Ronald reportedly called his brother and told him about the job opportunity.

Later, in the early morning hours of August 2, Ronald contacted his mother, **Mabel Jack**, in the Burns Lake area.

He reportedly explained that the family would be away for approximately **10 days to two weeks**.

The family expected to return before Russell needed to return to school.

Ronald also reportedly told his mother words to the effect of:

**If I don't come back, come looking for me.**

That statement has become one of the most chilling details of the case.

# **The Final Known Sighting**

After the telephone calls, Ronald, Doreen and the boys began preparing to leave.

Relatives of Doreen, including her sister **Lorene**, reportedly witnessed the family packing belongings.

At approximately **1:21 a.m. on August 2, 1989**, the four family members were seen leaving their home on **Strathcona Avenue**.

They entered the unidentified man's dark-coloured four-wheel-drive pickup.

The truck then left the area.

That is the last confirmed sighting of the entire Jack family.

# **Why the Family Did Not Immediately Get Reported Missing**

The family had told relatives that they expected to be away for roughly 10 days to two weeks.

Therefore, when they did not immediately call home, relatives initially had reason to believe that the family was simply working at the camp.

But the days became weeks.

No telephone call came.

No message arrived.

The family did not return.

Eventually, Mabel Jack became seriously concerned.

The family was officially reported missing on **August 25, 1989**.

# **The Empty Home**

When investigators examined the family's residence, a particularly important detail emerged.

The family had left behind much of their normal life.

Furniture remained.

Clothing remained.

The children's school records remained.

The circumstances suggested that they had intended to return.

This became an important argument against the theory that the family had simply decided to abandon their lives in Prince George and disappear voluntarily.

# **The Investigation's Early Problems**

The investigation became controversial almost immediately.

One of the strangest events occurred on **September 7, 1989**.

The Prince George RCMP mistakenly reported that the family had been found safe.

The report was incorrect.

The family had not been found.

The investigation was temporarily closed because of the error and was later reopened.

Historical newspaper records and later case reporting document the false “family is safe” report.

For the family, this mistake became another source of frustration.

# **The Family's Concerns About the Investigation**

Members of the Jack family have spoken publicly over the years about their concerns regarding how the disappearance was handled.

During the National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls, members of the Jack family and their community discussed the case and broader concerns regarding policing and the treatment of Indigenous families.

These accounts are important because they show how the disappearance was experienced by the family itself.

They should not, however, be confused with a court finding that police intentionally mishandled the case because of racism.

The documented fact is that the family has raised serious concerns about the investigation and about the treatment of Indigenous people in the region.

# **The 1990 CrimeStoppers Reconstruction**

In 1990, CrimeStoppers produced a televised reconstruction of the disappearance.

The purpose was to increase public awareness and encourage people with information to come forward.

A composite description of the unidentified man was also circulated.

Despite this effort, the investigation did not produce a confirmed location for the family.

# **The Reward**

Public efforts continued to keep the case alive.

By 1992, the **Prince George Native Friendship Centre** and Mabel Jack were involved in efforts to raise money and increase publicity.

CrimeStoppers was offering a reward of up to **$2,000** for information leading to the whereabouts of the family.

The reward and publicity did not produce a verified breakthrough.

# **The 1996 Anonymous Phone Call**

Nearly seven years after the disappearance, investigators received one of the strangest tips in the entire case.

On the morning of **January 28, 1996**, an anonymous man called the RCMP/Vanderhoof police.

The call lasted only about **10 seconds**.

The caller reportedly said that:

**“The Jack family are buried at the south end”**

of an unidentified ranch.

The name of the ranch was unclear on the recording.

The caller immediately hung up.

He did not provide:

* His name
* His location
* An explanation
* Details about the family
* Details about how he knew
* A precise burial location

Police tried to identify him.

The call was traced to a house in the **Stoney Creek/Vanderhoof area**, where a party had reportedly been taking place.

Investigators were unable to establish who exactly made the call.

The caller never came forward.

Police later appealed publicly for him to contact investigators again.

# **Was the 1996 Call Genuine?**

Nobody knows.

There are several possibilities.

### **Possibility 1 — The caller knew something**

Perhaps someone at the party actually knew what had happened to the Jack family.

If true, the caller may have been:

* A witness
* An accomplice
* Someone who had heard the information from another person
* Someone who knew the location of the bodies

### **Possibility 2 — It was a cruel hoax**

The party environment also creates another possibility.

Someone may simply have made the call as a prank.

Because the ranch name could not be clearly established and the caller disappeared, investigators could not verify the information.

### **Possibility 3 — The caller was intoxicated**

Another possibility is that the caller had genuine information but was intoxicated and unable or unwilling to explain it properly.

There is no public evidence proving any of these explanations.

# **The Ranch Theory**

The anonymous call created a major theory:

**The family may have been killed and buried somewhere near a ranch west of Prince George.**

Police investigated locations connected with the information.

But no remains were found.

The ranch described in the call has never produced a confirmed answer.

This is why the 1996 call remains intriguing but should **not** be described as proof that the family was murdered.

It is an unverified tip.

# **The 2018 Appeal to the Anonymous Caller**

In August 2019, investigators conducted one of the most significant modern searches.

# **What Happened to the Family?**

There is no confirmed answer.

However, the circumstances have led many investigators, relatives and observers to believe that the family did **not simply disappear voluntarily**.

Several facts support that concern:

* They told relatives they would return.
* Ronald specifically described a short-term job.
* The family left behind much of their property.
* The children's school records remained.
* They entered a vehicle belonging to an unidentified man.
* The supposed job location was never clearly established.
* Nobody has reliably heard from the family since.
* A later anonymous caller claimed they were buried.

None of these facts individually proves murder.

Together, however, they make the case highly suspicious.

# **Major Theory 1 — The Job Offer Was a Trap**

This is the most widely accepted theory among people who study the case.

The theory is simple:

The unidentified man knew that Ronald needed work.

He offered Ronald and Doreen an attractive employment arrangement.

He offered childcare for their children.

He offered transportation.

Then he convinced the entire family to leave with him at night.

The theory is that the job never existed and was simply a method of getting the family into his vehicle.

This theory is particularly compelling because the family was reportedly vulnerable financially and Ronald was actively looking for work.

A 2013 Highway of Tears symposium report specifically discussed the Jack family as an example of how employment could potentially be used as a lure against vulnerable First Nations families.

**Evidence level: Strong circumstantial theory, but not legally proven.**

# **Major Theory 2 — The Family Was Murdered**

Another major theory is that Ronald, Doreen, Russell and Ryan were killed shortly after leaving Prince George.

The 1996 anonymous call strongly influenced this theory because the caller claimed that the family had been buried.

The family's disappearance also resembles a scenario in which an entire family could have been taken to an isolated location.

But no bodies have ever been found.

There is also no publicly established murder scene.

Therefore, the theory cannot be proven.

**Evidence level: Plausible but unproven.**

# **Major Theory 3 — The Unidentified Man Was Responsible**

This is probably the most commonly discussed suspect theory.

The man was the last known outsider to interact with Ronald and was reportedly the person who transported the entire family away from Prince George.

The supposed job was never confirmed.

The family disappeared immediately afterward.

The man has never been identified.

That makes him the central figure in almost every reconstruction of the case.

However, there is no public evidence establishing exactly what happened after the family entered his truck.

**Evidence level: Major investigative lead, but no proven guilt.**

# **Major Theory 4 — The Job Was Real, But Something Happened Later**

A less sinister possibility is that the job offer was genuine but something happened afterward.

For example:

* A vehicle accident
* An accidental death
* A conflict at a remote work site
* A criminal incident unrelated to the original job offer
* The family becoming victims of someone they encountered afterward

There is no evidence confirming these scenarios.

They remain possibilities rather than established explanations.

# **Major Theory 5 — The Family Voluntarily Disappeared**

Some early speculation suggested that the family may have intentionally left Prince George.

Perhaps they wanted to start over somewhere else.

However, this theory faces major problems.

The family reportedly told relatives where they were going.

They said they would return after approximately 10 days to two weeks.

They left behind important belongings and school records.

And there has never been a confirmed sighting of any family member afterward.

For these reasons, voluntary disappearance is generally considered a weaker explanation.

# **Major Theory 6 — Financial Problems or Debts**

Ronald reportedly had financial problems around the time of the disappearance.

Some accounts state that he told his mother he owed money to someone.

This has led to speculation that the disappearance could have involved people connected to debt.

However, no publicly established evidence has identified a creditor or connected financial problems to the family's disappearance.

This should therefore be presented only as a possible investigative angle, not as a proven motive.

# **Major Theory 7 — Connection to the Highway of Tears**

The family disappeared in northern British Columbia near **Highway 16**, a corridor that later became widely known as the **Highway of Tears** because of numerous disappearances and killings, particularly involving Indigenous women and girls.

Because the Jack family disappeared in this broader geographical region, researchers and the public have sometimes discussed the case in that context.

The connection is important historically and socially.

However, there is **no established evidence that the Jack family's disappearance was connected to a specific Highway of Tears serial killer or another known case**.

The Highway of Tears association should therefore be treated as context, not proof of a connection.

# **Major Theory 8 — The 1996 Caller Was the Killer or an Accomplice**

One of the most interesting theories concerns the anonymous caller.

If the call was genuine, the caller may have known:

* That the family had died
* Where their bodies were buried
* That investigators were still searching
* Possibly who was responsible

Some people have speculated that the caller could have been:

* The perpetrator
* An accomplice

The caller's identity remains unknown.

# **Major Theory 9 — The 1996 Call Was a Hoax**

The opposite theory is that someone at the party simply decided to make a cruel prank call.

The call was extremely short.

The supposed ranch name was unclear.

The caller never called back.

Police traced the call to a house where a party was occurring, but could not establish exactly who made it.

# **Why the Case Is So Difficult to Solve**

The disappearance happened in 1989.

That creates enormous investigative problems.

There was:

* No widespread CCTV
* No mobile-phone location data
* No GPS tracking
* No modern digital payment trail
* Limited electronic records
* No social-media history
* No modern DNA evidence from a known crime scene

The RCMP itself has acknowledged that the lack of modern digital evidence makes this investigation particularly dependent on people coming forward with information.

After more than three decades, witnesses may have died, forgotten details or moved away.

Potential physical evidence may have deteriorated.

Locations may have changed dramatically.

And the person who knows what happened may simply never have spoken.
